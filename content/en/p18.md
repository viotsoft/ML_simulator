# Module P18. Risk, fairness and compliance

> **Level:** Senior ML PM · **Time:** ~55 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

Two weeks after photo moderation went to full ramp, the appeals dashboard shows something you cannot ignore. Sellers in one region are having photos rejected **three times more often** than sellers elsewhere.

Dmitry, counsel, gets there first:

> "Before anyone explains this to me, I need to know one thing. Is the model wrong more often for those sellers, or are those sellers actually breaking the rules more often? Those are completely different conversations, and only one of them is a problem."

He is right, and the distinction is the entire module. A support lead in the same thread is already proposing a fix — *"lower the threshold for that region"* — which would be the correct action for one of the two explanations and a serious mistake for the other.

## 🎯 Your task

1. Name the harm rather than the abstraction.
2. Measure error rates by group, not outcome rates.
3. Know what has to exist on paper before an automated decision affects anyone.

---

## 📚 Theory

### Name the harm

"Bias" is too vague to act on. Say who is harmed and how, and the work becomes tractable. Four kinds cover most product cases:

- **Wrongful denial.** Someone is refused something they were entitled to — a listing, a payment, an account.
- **Unequal error rates.** The system is wrong more often about one group of people than another.
- **Inference of something sensitive.** A model that does not use a protected attribute can still infer it, and act on it.
- **Safety.** Physical or financial harm from a wrong automated action.

This is the single most useful idea in the module, because it protects you in both directions. It stops you from shipping a real harm you dismissed as a base-rate difference, and it stops you from "fixing" a legitimate difference by lowering the bar for one group — which harms buyers, and harms the honest sellers in that region most of all.

Getting the second chart costs work: a sample of decisions from each group, re-reviewed blind by a senior human. There is no way around this. The error rate cannot be computed from production data alone, because production data does not know which rejections were wrong.

### Risk tiers decide the paperwork, not the algorithm

The same technique is unremarkable in one product and heavily regulated in another. What raises the tier is the consequence for the person:

- **Low:** ranking, recommendations, internal forecasting.
- **Medium:** content moderation, pricing, fraud screening — money and access are affected, and appeals matter.
- **High:** credit, employment, housing, insurance, health, anything involving minors or biometrics. Documentation, human oversight and explanation are typically legal requirements rather than good practice.

Work out the tier at the PRD stage, not at the launch review. The cost of a high tier is entirely front-loaded, and discovering it late is what turns a two-week delay into a quarter.

### Five things that exist before launch

1. **A written purpose.** What this system decides, for whom, and on what basis.
2. **The error-rate breakdown** across the groups you can lawfully measure.
3. **A human review path** with a response time, from module P9.
4. **A record of decisions** — inputs, output, model version, timestamp — from module P13. Without it you cannot answer a complaint or a regulator.
5. **A named owner.** A person, not a team.

### The duty to explain has a product shape

Telling someone a decision was automated, giving a reason they can act on, and offering a route to a human is a legal requirement in a growing number of places — and it is also just the difference between a product and an insult. "Photo rejected" fails all three tests. Module P9's copy passes them.

### Some errors are not traded against money

Module P6's floor belongs here. When the harm is to safety, dignity or a protected group, you do not price it against fraud losses — you set a limit you will not ship above, record it as a policy, and name who may change it. The reason to write it down as a policy is that an invented monetary figure can be renegotiated by anyone with a spreadsheet, and a stated limit cannot.

## 💻 Practice

Sellers in region B have photos rejected 3× more often. What do you do, in order?

<details>
<summary><b>Show a model answer</b></summary>

**1. Refuse both explanations until there is a number.** Neither "they break the rules more" nor "the model is biased" is knowable from the rejection rate. Say so plainly, and do not let the threshold be changed in the meantime.

**2. Buy the second chart.** Take 200 automatic rejections from region B and 200 from region A, strip any region marker, and have two senior moderators review them blind. This takes about two days and is the only way the error rate exists.

Suppose it comes back: wrongful rejection **1.1%** in A, **3.4%** in B. That is a real disparity in errors, not in behaviour, and it is now a finding rather than an argument.

**3. Find the mechanism.** Reading the wrong cases — module P11's twenty errors — the pattern is concrete: sellers in region B photograph products on patterned fabric rather than a white background, which is the local marketplace convention, and the training data is overwhelmingly studio photos on white. The model is not reacting to the region. It is reacting to a background it has barely seen, and region is what that correlates with.

**4. Act in this order.**

- **Stop automating for the affected pattern today.** Those photos route to human review instead of automatic rejection. Accuracy drops, review volume rises, and both are the correct price.
- **Re-review the past rejections** in the affected window and restore the ones that were wrong — proactively, not on appeal. Waiting for appeals means the remedy reaches only the sellers who knew how to complain, which compounds the original harm.
- **Fix the cause:** collect and label photos with varied backgrounds, retrain, and verify on a test set built specifically to contain them.
- **Add a launch criterion:** the wrongful-rejection gap between any two measured segments stays within an agreed band, checked before every model promotion. This is now part of module P10's gate.

**5. Do not lower the threshold for region B.** It was the wrong fix all along: it would have hidden the error-rate gap by letting genuine violations through in that region, which harms buyers there and the honest sellers competing with those violations. The right fix targets the cause; a per-region threshold targets the symptom and calls it fairness.

**6. Write it down.** One page: what was measured, what was found, what was done, who owns it, when it is re-checked. This page is what turns an incident into something an auditor, a regulator or your successor can follow — and it costs an hour today against weeks of reconstruction later.

**What you say to Dmitry.** *"The model is wrong more often for those sellers, by about three times. It is not using region — it is using background, and the two travel together. Automation is off for that pattern as of today, past rejections are being restored, and the fix has a test set. You will have the page by Friday."*
</details>

## 📌 Remember

- Name the harm: wrongful denial, unequal error rates, inference of something sensitive, or safety. "Bias" is not actionable.
- Outcome rates and error rates are different questions. A group that breaks a rule more often is legitimately rejected more often; the finding is whether the system is *mistaken* more often.
- The error rate cannot be computed from production data. It costs a blind re-review of a sample, and there is no way around that.
- Risk tier is set by the consequence for the person, and it decides the documentation. Work it out at the PRD stage — a late discovery turns two weeks into a quarter.
- Five things before launch: a written purpose, the error-rate breakdown, a human review path, a record of every decision, and a named owner.
- Fix the mechanism, not the symptom. A per-group threshold usually hides the harm and calls it fairness.
- Remedy proactively. Waiting for appeals delivers the remedy only to people who knew how to complain.
