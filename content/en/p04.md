# Module P4. Does the data even exist?

> **Level:** PM Foundations · **Time:** ~50 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

The delivery promise is in the quarter. The idea is straightforward: at checkout, tell the customer honestly whether the order is likely to arrive late, instead of showing the same optimistic window to everyone.

You take it to Anya, who owns the data. She does not say no. She asks four questions, and the third one nearly kills the feature:

> "History of deliveries — 1.2 million of them, sure. Late or not late — we can work that out from the actual delivery time, yes.
>
> But what do you know **at checkout**? Not the courier, we assign that after the order is placed. Not the warehouse load at dispatch time. Not the weather on the delivery day. Half the columns that would make this model good are things we only learn afterwards.
>
> So: what is actually on the table at the moment you want to show the customer a number?"

This is the conversation that should happen before a feature reaches a roadmap, and almost never does.

## 🎯 Your task

1. Run the four-question data audit before committing to anything.
2. Understand the point-in-time trap — the most expensive mistake on this list.
3. Know where labels come from and what they cost.

---

## 📚 Theory

### Four questions, in this order

**1. Does the data exist at all?** Not "could we collect it" — does it exist today, in a place someone can query. If the answer is no, your feature has a data-collection project in front of it, and that project has its own quarter.

**2. Is it labelled?** The model learns from examples of the right answer. For the delivery promise the label is easy: we know when the order actually arrived. For "is this photo in the right category" someone has to look at photos and say. That is human work, it has a price, and it belongs in the estimate.

**3. Is it available at the moment of the decision?** Anya's question. This one is covered below because it is where features die quietly.

**4. Whose is it, and may we use it for this?** Another team's table can be changed without telling you. Personal data carries consent and retention rules. If the data came from a partner, the contract may say what it can be used for — module P17 goes into this.

### The point-in-time trap

A model is trained on history, where everything is already known. It runs on the present, where most of it is not.

If a column exists in the training table but not at the moment of prediction, the model learns to rely on it and then loses it in production. The metric looks excellent offline and collapses live. The ML track has the post-mortem of exactly this failure, and module P11 gives you the questions that catch it in review.

The practical version for a product manager: **draw the timeline**. Mark the moment of the decision. Everything to the left of it is allowed; everything to the right is a leak, no matter how predictive it looks.

### Where labels come from

Three sources, in descending order of joy:

**The system already records the answer.** Delivery time, whether the payment was charged back, whether the customer came back. Free, plentiful, and the reason these features are the cheap ones to start with.

**A person labels it.** Someone reviews photos, transcripts, tickets. Real money and real calendar time, and quality varies with how well the instructions are written. Budget it explicitly, because it is the line most often left out of an estimate.

**The label is a proxy.** You wanted "did the customer find this useful" and you settle for "did they click". Proxies are normal and often unavoidable — but every proxy quietly changes what the model optimises. A click is not usefulness, and the gap between them is where recommendation systems learn to bait people.

### Coverage: the question after the four

Even when data exists, it rarely covers everything. New products have no history. New customers have no behaviour. A model trained on the busy part of the catalogue will be confidently wrong on the tail.

This is a product decision, not a technical one: either you scope the feature to where the data is good and say so out loud, or you design what happens where it is not. Silence produces the worst outcome — a feature that works in the demo and embarrasses you on the long tail.

## 💻 Practice

Three ideas are on the table for next quarter. Run the four questions on each, and say what you would do.

> **A.** Show a "likely to be late" warning at checkout.
>
> **B.** Auto-reject seller photos that do not match the chosen category.
>
> **C.** Predict which support ticket will escalate to a complaint, so the team can pick it up first.

<details>
<summary><b>Show a model answer</b></summary>

**A. Exists yes, labelled yes, available — partially, ours yes.** The label is free and 1.2 million rows is plenty. The catch is question three: the strongest signals appear after checkout. **Do it, scoped honestly** to what is known at checkout, and expect a weaker model than the offline experiment on the full table would suggest. Tell the team that up front, or they will build against columns you cannot ship.

**B. Exists yes, labelled no, available yes, ours yes.** The photos are there; nobody has ever said which ones are miscategorised. This project contains a labelling project. **Get a price for labelling before committing** — a few thousand reviewed photos is a normal starting point, and it is a line in the estimate, not a detail.

**C. Exists yes, labelled almost, available yes, ours — with a caveat.** "Escalated to a complaint" is recorded, so the label is nearly free. The caveat is question four: support tickets carry personal data, and using them to train needs the retention and consent story checked. **Do it, with legal in the loop early** rather than at launch review.

The pattern: none of the three was rejected, and all three changed shape. That is what the audit is for.
</details>

## 📌 Remember

- Four questions, in order: does it exist, is it labelled, is it available at the moment of the decision, and may we use it. Ask them before the roadmap, not after.
- The point-in-time trap is the expensive one. Draw the timeline, mark the decision, and treat everything to its right as a leak — however predictive it looks.
- Labels may come from the future. Features may not.
- Labelling by humans is money and calendar time. If it is in the project, it is in the estimate.
- Where the data is thin, either scope the feature to where it is good and say so, or design what happens on the tail. Silence picks the worst option for you.
