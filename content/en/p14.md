# Module P14. Life after launch

> **Level:** ML PM · **Time:** ~50 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

Three months after the launch. You open the monthly review deck and stop at the complaints chart: late-delivery complaints are back to where they were before the feature existed.

Nothing has broken. Every dashboard is green — error rate normal, latency normal, the service has not had an incident in eleven weeks. Nobody was paged, because nothing that anybody monitored went wrong.

Aleksandra from data engineering finds it in twenty minutes:

> "Two cities switched courier partner in month two. The new courier's identifier never appeared in the training data, so the model treats it as unknown and predicts close to the average. About a third of our orders have effectively had the feature switched off since week six."

Nobody switched anything off. The world moved, and the model stayed where it was.

## 🎯 Your task

1. Monitor the things that are visible immediately, not just the things that are true.
2. Make the retraining cadence a decision instead of a habit.
3. Build a dashboard you will actually read, with triggers attached.

---

## 📚 Theory

### You cannot measure accuracy today

This is the fact that shapes everything else. The truth about a prediction arrives later: two days for a delivery, forty-five for a chargeback, ninety for churn. So "how accurate is the model right now" is not a question that has an answer — which is why monitoring built only from accuracy monitors nothing at all during exactly the window when something is going wrong.

### Four numbers, in this order

1. **The business metric.** Complaints per 1,000 orders, weekly, with the pre-launch level drawn on the same chart. Slow, noisy, and the only one that matters.
2. **The prediction rate.** The share of cases the model acts on, daily. If it moves and there was no release, *the world moved.* This is the cheapest and fastest early warning that exists, and it is the line that was missing in the situation above.
3. **The input health rate.** The share of predictions made with a missing, unseen or out-of-range input. This is the number that names the cause once line 2 raises the alarm.
4. **Accuracy on matured labels.** Precision and recall at the live threshold, on cases old enough to have an outcome, weekly. True and late.

And one line that is not a metric but prevents a week of confusion: **the model version and the date of the last retrain.** Without it you cannot tell a change from a drift.

### Drift is not one thing

- **The inputs changed.** A new courier, a renamed category, a form that now allows an empty field. Usually abrupt, usually caused by a release somewhere else in the company, and detectable in hours.
- **The world changed.** Customer behaviour in December, a competitor's promotion, a new fee. Gradual, real, and not anybody's bug.
- **The feedback loop bit you.** The model's own actions changed the data it is trained on next — orders it advised got rescheduled, so they no longer look late. Module P17 covers this properly; it is the drift that is hardest to see because the numbers keep looking good.

### Retraining cadence is a decision, not a default

Retraining weekly because it sounds diligent has a price: every retrain produces a *new model* that can be worse, and it needs the same evaluation and the same gate as the first one. Automatic retraining with no gate is a pipeline that can quietly ship a regression every Monday.

Set the cadence from the measured drift: retrain when line 4 falls by more than an agreed amount, or on a schedule derived from how fast it actually decayed over the last two quarters. Then keep the gate — a new model that does not beat the one in production does not get promoted just because it is newer.

### Turning a feature off is part of the lifecycle

The cost of owning a model is monthly and permanent, and the value can fade. If the rule has caught up, if the increment no longer covers the machinery from module P10, or if the decision it feeds no longer exists, then switching it off is good product management and not an admission of anything.

## 💻 Practice

Design the monitoring that would have caught this in week six instead of month three, and say what each trigger does.

**Four lines and one label, with a trigger on each.**

| # | What | Cadence | Trigger | Who acts |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Late-delivery complaints per 1,000 orders, with the pre-launch line drawn | weekly | above the pre-launch level for two weeks | product review |
| 2 | Share of orders advised | daily | ±30% relative to the launch baseline with no release that week | alert to product and the on-call engineer |
| 3 | Share of predictions with a missing or unseen input value | daily | above 2% | alert to data engineering |
| 4 | Recall and precision at the live threshold, on orders delivered 7+ days ago | weekly | recall more than 5 points below its launch value | schedule a retrain, run the gate |
| — | Model version and last retrain date | on the chart | — | — |

**Which one would have caught it.** Line 2, within days. A third of orders losing the advisory drops the national flag rate by roughly a third — far outside a ±30% band, in week six, with no release to explain it. Line 3 would then have named the cause in one click: unseen courier identifier, two cities.

Line 4 could not have caught it in week six even in principle: the deliveries had not finished, and by the time the labels matured the number would have been a small, ambiguous dip against seasonal noise.

**The two fixes, and they are different.**

- **Immediate:** map the new courier to the nearest known one, or retrain including the new data. This makes the feature work again.
- **Structural:** an unseen categorical value must not silently become an average prediction. It must count as *no prediction* — which, by module P9, means the silent band, the advisory is not shown, and line 3 goes up. The feature failing visibly is worth far more than the feature failing quietly.

**What this incident actually cost.** Eleven weeks of a third of the traffic getting no feature, and a monthly review where the headline number moved and nobody could explain it. Nothing broke; that was the problem. Every dashboard in the company was measuring whether the service was up, and none of them was measuring whether the feature was doing anything.

**One sentence for the retro.** A model that stops working does not throw an error — it keeps answering, confidently, with the average.

## 📌 Remember

- Accuracy today is unknowable: labels arrive days or months later. Monitoring built only on accuracy is blind during the exact window when something goes wrong.
- Four numbers: the business metric, the prediction rate, the input health rate, and accuracy on matured labels. The prediction rate is the cheapest early warning there is.
- Put the model version and last retrain date on the chart, or you cannot tell a change from a drift.
- Drift has three sources — the inputs changed, the world changed, or the model's own actions changed its future training data — and only the first is anybody's bug.
- Retraining cadence follows from measured drift, and every retrained model passes the same gate. Automatic retraining with no gate can ship a regression every Monday.
- An unseen input must produce no prediction rather than an average one. Failing visibly is worth more than failing quietly.
- Switching a feature off when the increment no longer covers its cost is good product management.
