# Module P20. Capstone: the review board

> **Level:** Senior ML PM · **Time:** ~70 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

Thursday, 10 a.m. Viktor, Marina and Dmitry in one room, forty minutes, one decision: does **Smart Refunds** ship?

Here is everything you have.

> **The feature.** Customers request a refund; today every request is read by a person. The model predicts whether a claim is legitimate, and above a threshold the refund is approved automatically. **It never denies a refund automatically** — every rejection goes to a person.
>
> **Volume and cost.** 140,000 requests a month, handled by a team of 88 at about **$1.80 a request**: **$252,000 a month.** About **9%** of claims are not legitimate. An average refund is **$28**.
>
> **At the proposed threshold.** 62% of requests are auto-approved — **86,800 a month** that no person touches. On that same slice, a blind re-review found the model wrongly approves **3.1%** of claims, and the agents it replaces wrongly approve **4.4%**.
>
> **Inference** costs about $112 a month. A **1.5% hold-out** of auto-approvals is sent to an agent anyway, purely to keep measuring.

Viktor has one question he always asks. Marina has one she always asks. Dmitry has one you have not heard yet. You have forty minutes and one page.

## 🎯 Your task

1. Write the one page that a CTO, a finance lead and a lawyer can all read.
2. Name your own weakest number before anybody else finds it.
3. State what would change your mind, in a number.

---

## 📚 Theory

### A defence has six blocks

You are not presenting a model. You are asking three people with different fears to accept a decision. Each block answers one of those fears, in their language.

1. **The decision.** What changes, at what moment, for whom, and who acts on it. Module P3.
2. **The money, both directions, with the baseline next to it.** Not the model's benefit in isolation — the increment over what happens today. Modules P10 and P15.
3. **The two errors, and which one you refuse to automate.** Module P6, and the floor from P18.
4. **The fallback and the rollback.** What the product does with no model, and how it is switched off in a minute. Module P13.
5. **The risk tier and what exists on paper.** Module P18.
6. **What would change your mind**, expressed as a number somebody could go and measure.

This is the single most transferable idea in the module. When the two errors are unequal *in kind* rather than merely in price, the answer is often not a cleverer threshold — it is to automate one direction and leave the other with a person. You give up some of the saving and you remove the harm you could not have priced honestly.

### Name your weakest number first

Every proposal has one number that will not survive scrutiny. Say it yourself, in the first two minutes, with what you are doing about it. A finance lead who finds the soft number themselves stops believing the other five; a finance lead who hears it from you starts helping you firm it up.

### Smart Refunds — decision requested: ship at 5% canary Tuesday, full ramp in three weeks

**1. The decision.** At the moment a refund request is submitted, a claim the model judges clearly legitimate is approved immediately and the customer is told within seconds instead of within two days. Every other request goes to the refunds team, as today. The team stops reading 86,800 requests a month and reads the remaining 53,200.

**2. The money, against today's process.**

| | Per month |
| --- | --- |
| Agent time no longer spent (86,800 × $1.80) | **+$156,240** |
| Wrongly approved claims, model (3.1% × 86,800 × $28) | −$75,348 |
| Wrongly approved claims, agents on that same slice (4.4%) | *−$106,932 today* |
| So: error cost **falls** by | **+$31,584** |
| Inference | −$112 |
| 1.5% hold-out sent to an agent anyway | −$2,344 |
| **Net** | **≈ +$185,400 a month, about $2.2M a year** |

The model is cheaper *and* more accurate than the process it replaces on this slice — and the second claim only means anything because the 4.4% baseline was measured the same way, by blind re-review, rather than assumed.

**3. The two errors, and the one we will not automate.** A wrongly approved refund costs $28 and the company absorbs it. A wrongly denied refund takes money from a customer who was entitled to it. These are not the same kind of error, so only the first is automated. **No refund is ever denied by the model.** Refusals come from a person, with a reason and a one-click appeal answered within one business day.

**4. Fallback and rollback.** If the model is unavailable, everything routes to the team — which is today's process, and at full volume the queue grows by about 4,000 a day. So the fallback is not "do nothing": a rule approves claims under $10 from customers with two or more years of history and no claim in the last twelve months, covering about 18% of requests and keeping the queue survivable for a day. Rollback is a config flag, effective within a minute, flippable by anyone on call without an engineer.

**5. Risk tier and paperwork.** Medium. Money and access are affected, and appeals matter. In place before the canary: a written purpose; the wrongful-approval rate broken out by customer tenure, region and claim size; every decision logged with inputs, output and model version; the appeal path in code; and a named owner — me.

**6. What would change my mind.** The whole case rests on 3.1%. **The deal turns negative at a wrongful-approval rate of about 10.7%** — three and a half times what we measured. The 1.5% hold-out reports that rate weekly, and the flag goes off if it passes 7%.

---

### The three challenges

**Viktor: "What happens when it breaks at 3 a.m.?"**

> "Nothing wakes you. The flag goes to off, the fallback rule covers 18% of requests, and the rest queues for the team — which is exactly today's process. The runbook defines broken in numbers: auto-approval share moving more than 30% in a day, or the hold-out's wrongful-approval rate above 7%. Both alert to me and to the on-call engineer, and the switch is in the config dashboard, not in a deploy."

**Marina: "Your $2.2M assumes 3.1% holds. Does it?"**

> "That is the right question and it is my weakest number, so here it is first: 3.1% comes from a blind re-review of 400 auto-approvals, which gives me confidence in the first decimal place and no more. The important number is therefore not 3.1% but 10.7% — the rate at which this stops paying. We are three and a half times away from it. And the 1.5% hold-out, which costs $2,344 a month, is how we keep knowing: without it, precision would drift upwards on a shrinking slice of reality and tell us nothing, because approved claims never come back to teach us anything."

**Dmitry: "You are making automated decisions about people's money."**

> "One direction only, and it is the favourable one. The model never refuses anyone. Every refusal is a human decision with a stated reason and an appeal answered in one business day, and every automated approval is logged with its inputs and the model version, so any individual case can be reconstructed. The purpose is written down and limited to refund handling — this data does not move to pricing or marketing without a separate basis. And one commitment beyond the minimum: the wrongful-approval rate is reported by region, tenure and claim size, because if the model turns out to be more generous to one group than another, that is a finding I want to have before you ask for it."

### What this page does not contain

No model family. No feature list. No accuracy figure. Nothing any of the three people in the room would have to take on trust from a fourth who is not there — and nothing you could not defend a year from now.

## 📌 Remember

- A defence has six blocks: the decision, the money against today's baseline, the two errors and the one you refuse to automate, the fallback and rollback, the risk tier and paperwork, and what would change your mind.
- When two errors differ in *kind* and not just in price, automate one direction and leave the other with a person. You give up some saving and remove a harm you could not have priced honestly.
- A saving is only credible next to a baseline measured the same way. "Better than the humans" means nothing unless the humans were measured by the same blind re-review.
- Name your weakest number in the first two minutes, with what you are doing about it. Found by someone else, it discredits the other five.
- Express the limit of your case as a number somebody could measure: not "we are confident", but "this stops paying at 10.7%, and we are at 3.1%".
- The fallback is never "do nothing" when the manual process cannot absorb the volume. It is a rule, sized to keep the queue survivable.
- None of the six blocks mentions the model's architecture. If a document cannot be defended without it, it is the wrong document.
