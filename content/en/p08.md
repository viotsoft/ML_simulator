# Module P8. The PRD for an ML feature

> **Level:** ML PM · **Time:** ~50 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

You sent the delay-warning PRD to the team using the template that has served you for four years. Igor, the senior data scientist, sends it back with two comments.

Half of section 3 is struck through, with:

> "This part is not yours. If you fix the model type and the feature list now, I cannot use the better thing I find in week two — and you will be defending a choice you have no way to defend."

And in the margin of the interface section, one question:

> "What does the product do when the model is unsure? It will be unsure on about one order in six."

The template is not wrong. It was written for features that are certain, and this one is not.

## 🎯 Your task

1. Know the sections an ML PRD must have that an ordinary one does not.
2. Know which sections to leave out on purpose, and why writing them costs you.
3. Write acceptance criteria as behaviours rather than as accuracy.

---

## 📚 Theory

### The line runs between decisions and mechanisms

The PRD owns what the feature does to the business and to the user. The team owns how the prediction is produced. Crossing that line in either direction causes a specific failure: your version locks the team out of better options, and their version leaves nobody answerable for the cost of an error.

### The three sections that are new

**Behaviour when the model is not confident.** Not an edge case — the main case. A model that is unsure on one order in six needs a defined product behaviour for those orders, and "show the prediction anyway" is a decision you have to make on purpose rather than by omission. Module P9 is the whole design problem.

**The fallback.** What the product does when the model is unavailable, times out, or has just been rolled back. Every ML feature has a no-model mode, whether or not you designed it. If you did not, the no-model mode is an error page.

**The cost of both errors.** From module P6, in the document, in numbers. This is what lets the team choose a threshold without guessing at your preferences, and what lets you defend the threshold later to whoever is unhappy about it.

### The coverage limit is a section, not a footnote

Module P4's answer — the data exists for mobile orders only — belongs in the PRD as scope: *this feature is for mobile orders in these regions; web orders see the current behaviour.* Written down, it is a scope decision. Discovered at launch, it is a broken promise.

### Acceptance criteria are behaviours, not accuracy

"At least 90% accuracy" is not an acceptance criterion. It cannot be verified against a user-visible outcome, it says nothing about which error got better, and it turns the launch decision into a single number that module P5 already told you not to trust alone.

Criteria that can actually be accepted or rejected:

- No user sees a blocking error when the model times out; the page renders without the warning.
- Every automated rejection can be appealed in one click, and the appeal is answered within one business day.
- The warning is never shown as a certainty. The copy states a rate.
- Below the confidence floor, nothing is shown.
- The threshold is configurable without a deploy.

Each of these is testable by someone who knows nothing about machine learning, which is exactly the property an acceptance criterion needs.

## 💻 Practice

Here is section 3 of a real PRD. Find what is wrong with it and rewrite it.

> **3. Behaviour.** The system will use a gradient-boosted model over 40 features, including courier history and weather, to predict delivery delays with at least 90% accuracy. A red "Your order may be late" banner appears on the order page.

<details>
<summary><b>Show a model answer</b></summary>

**Three faults, and they are all in the product manager's half of the document.**

1. **It fixes the mechanism.** "Gradient-boosted, 40 features, including weather" is Igor's decision, and writing it costs you twice: the team cannot adopt something better without a PRD amendment, and in the launch review you will be asked to justify a choice you are not equipped to justify.
2. **Accuracy as the criterion.** 90% of what? A model that always predicts "on time" scores 90% if nine orders in ten arrive on time — and it never shows a warning, so the feature does nothing. There is no decision, no error cost, no metric chain.
3. **No unsure state, no fallback, no appeal.** One banner, one certainty, and nothing at all for the one order in six where the model is undecided or for the hour when the service is down.

**The rewrite.**

> **3. Behaviour.** At the moment the order page renders, the customer is shown a delay advisory when the risk of a late delivery is high.
>
> **The decision it changes:** the customer can pick a different delivery slot before paying.
>
> **Three states.** *High risk* — an advisory with a rate, not a certainty: "orders like this arrive late about 1 time in 4", plus a link to change the slot. *Uncertain* — nothing is shown. *No model available* — nothing is shown, and the page renders normally.
>
> **The cost of the two errors.** A missed delay costs a support contact and a refund request, on average $6. A false warning costs a slot change nobody needed, and occasionally an abandoned basket, about $2. The threshold follows from that ratio and is configurable without a deploy.
>
> **Metrics.** Business: late-delivery complaints per 1,000 orders. Online proxy: share of advised customers who change the slot. Offline: recall at the chosen threshold. **Deciding number:** complaints down 8%. **Guardrail:** checkout conversion does not drop by more than 0.3pp.
>
> **Scope.** Mobile orders in the three cities where courier history is recorded. Web orders keep the current behaviour. Extending to web needs the courier data first.
>
> **Acceptance.** No blocking error on model timeout. The copy never states a certainty. Nothing is shown below the confidence floor. The threshold is changeable without a deploy.

The rewrite is longer and yet says less about the model than the original did — and every line in it is something you can still defend in six months.
</details>

## 📌 Remember

- The PRD owns decisions and consequences; the team owns mechanisms. Writing their half locks them out of better options and leaves you defending what you cannot defend.
- Three sections an ordinary PRD does not have: behaviour when the model is unsure, the fallback when there is no model, and the cost of both errors in numbers.
- The unsure state is the main case, not an edge case. Showing the prediction anyway is a decision, not a default.
- Every ML feature has a no-model mode. Undesigned, that mode is an error page.
- The coverage limit is a scope section. Written down it is a decision; discovered at launch it is a broken promise.
- Acceptance criteria are behaviours a non-specialist can test. "90% accuracy" is not one.
