# Module P16. Build, buy, or call an API

> **Level:** Senior ML PM · **Time:** ~55 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

Viktor forwards you a vendor deck with three words: *"Thoughts by Friday?"*

The vendor sells fraud detection as a service. Two cents a transaction, live in three weeks, and a claim on slide four: **75% of fraud caught**, against the 62% your own model manages.

Igor's reaction arrives four minutes later:

> "Or I spend three weeks improving ours. Also — they want a feed of every transaction we process. Has anyone asked Dmitry about that?"

Marina's arrives six minutes later, and is one line: *"cheaper or more expensive?"*

Three people are asking three different questions, and only one of them is the question that decides.

## 🎯 Your task

1. Decide whether the capability is a differentiator or a utility before comparing prices.
2. Find the four costs of buying that never appear in the quote.
3. Treat a vendor's headline number as a hypothesis about your data.

---

## 📚 Theory

### The first question is not cost

Ask what the capability *is*.

- **A utility** is something the whole industry needs in the same form: transcription, translation, optical character recognition, generic content safety. You will never out-invest the companies whose entire business is that one thing, and building it yourself buys no advantage even if you succeed.
- **A differentiator** is something that is only valuable because of *your* data and *your* context: the fraud patterns of your particular wallet, your search ranking, your pricing. Here the model is not the asset — the data is, and it is not for sale.

A quick classification, to make the axis concrete:

| Capability | What it is | Default |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Transcribing support calls | utility | call an API |
| Fraud patterns of our wallet | differentiator | build |
| Ranking our search results | differentiator | build |
| Writing product copy from a photo | utility | call an API, revisit at volume |

### The four costs that are not in the quote

1. **Integration.** Weeks of engineering, plus a new failure mode in module P13's runbook.
2. **Your data leaves.** Every transaction goes to a third party — which is a legal question before it is an architectural one, and module P18's paperwork applies.
3. **The switching cost.** Six months from now, moving away means rebuilding what you never built.
4. **The ceiling.** When the vendor is wrong in a way specific to your business, you cannot fix it. You can file a request. A false-block pattern that costs you $30,000 a month becomes somebody else's backlog item.

### Lock-in is a risk with a written answer

Ask the question out loud and record the reply: **what do we do if they double the price, get acquired, or shut down in eighteen months?** Acceptable answers include "we keep our own model running in shadow as an exit route" and "we can switch to two named alternatives in a month". An unacceptable answer is silence, and the time it costs nothing to ask is before signing.

### What a foundation-model API changed

Build-or-buy used to be binary, and now there is a third option with different properties: call a general-purpose model with your own prompt and context. Near-zero build cost, working in days, priced per call, and — this is the part that matters strategically — **no moat.** Your competitor can call the same model tomorrow.

That makes it an exceptional way to *find out whether the decision matters at all*, which is module P7's spike with the search removed, and a poor permanent home for a differentiator at scale, where module P15's per-call arithmetic catches up with you.

### The vendor's number is a hypothesis about your data

"75% of fraud caught" was measured on somebody else's transactions, by someone with an interest in the result. It is not a lie and it is not evidence. The only number that counts is their model's performance on *your* traffic, at *your* alert budget — which module P13 already gave you a way to obtain: run them in shadow.

## 💻 Practice

Answer Viktor. Here is what you have.

> 1.1M transactions a month. The vendor asks **$0.02** each — **$22,000 a month**. Your own model costs about **$4,000 a month** all in.
>
> Fraud: about **1,800 transactions a month**, each missed one costing **$220**. Your model catches **62%**. The vendor claims **75%** — measured on their other customers.
>
> They want a live feed of every transaction. Contract: 24 months, price fixed for 12.

<details>
<summary><b>Show a model answer</b></summary>

**Do the arithmetic first, because it is not what Igor expects.**

- Missed fraud today: 38% of 1,800 = 684 × $220 = **$150,480 a month.**
- Missed fraud at 75%: 450 × $220 = **$99,000 a month.** A saving of **$51,480.**
- Extra cost: $22,000 − $4,000 = **$18,000 a month.**
- **Net, if the 75% is real: +$33,480 a month.** About $400,000 a year.

So the vendor is not an expensive convenience. On their numbers this is a good deal, and "fraud is our differentiator" is not on its own an argument worth $400,000 a year.

**But their number is about their customers, not ours.** Our fraud mix, our device population, our geography. It is entirely normal for a vendor model to land at 60% on a new customer's traffic — in which case the deal is $18,000 a month for nothing.

**The answer to Viktor, in three parts.**

1. **Do not sign, do not refuse. Measure.** One month in shadow mode on our live traffic: they score every transaction, act on nothing, and we compare catch rate at *our* alert budget of 500 a day. The criterion is written before we start: **70% or better on our traffic, at our alert volume.** Below that, no deal. This is module P13's shadow stage used as a purchasing instrument.
2. **Price the exit before signing.** Our own model keeps running, in shadow, on the vendor's dime as far as the business case goes — it is about 2% of the saving and it is the whole difference between a supplier and a dependency. The contract needs an exit at 12 months, not 24, and the data feed needs Dmitry's sign-off on purpose, retention and deletion on termination before anything leaves our systems.
3. **What we are actually buying.** Not a model — thirteen points of catch rate. If in month two our own team finds seven of those points for three weeks of work, the deal is worth much less, and that comparison belongs in the same document.

**What you say to Marina.** *"On their numbers, $33,000 a month in our favour. On numbers measured on our traffic — which nobody has yet — unknown. One month of shadow testing decides a $400,000-a-year question, and it costs us nothing but the integration we would need anyway."*

**And the sentence that keeps the decision honest.** We are buying a utility-shaped delivery of a differentiator-shaped capability. That is acceptable while we keep our own model alive and our exit priced — and stops being acceptable the moment either of those quietly lapses.

## 📌 Remember

- Decide what the capability is before comparing prices: a utility you will never out-invest, or a differentiator whose value comes from your data.
- Four costs are missing from every quote: integration, your data leaving, the switching cost, and the ceiling on fixing what is specific to you.
- Ask what happens if the vendor doubles the price, is acquired, or shuts down — and write the answer down before signing.
- A foundation-model API is a third option: fast, per-call, and with no moat. Excellent for finding out whether the decision matters; poor as a permanent home for a differentiator at scale.
- A vendor's headline number was measured on somebody else's data by someone with an interest in it. Shadow mode turns it into evidence.
- Buying a differentiator can be right when the arithmetic is large enough — provided your own capability stays alive and your exit stays priced.
