# Module P6. What a wrong prediction costs

> **Level:** PM Foundations · **Time:** ~50 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

The wallet's fraud model is ready. The meeting to set the threshold has two people in it who want opposite things.

Risk:

> "Every fraudulent transaction we let through is money out of a customer's account and a chargeback for us. Set it aggressive."

Support:

> "Every honest customer we block calls us, and half of them do not come back. We had one last month who was buying a wedding present. Set it conservative."

Both are right. Both are describing a real cost. Neither can settle it, because the argument is about a trade, and a trade needs numbers on both sides.

Igor, from the ML team, says the part that puts it on your desk:

> "I can give you the model at any threshold you want. Which one do you want is not a question I can answer — it decides how many honest people we upset in order to catch how much fraud. That is your call."

## 🎯 Your task

1. Put a number on each of the two errors, even a rough one.
2. Turn the cost of errors and the team's capacity into a threshold.
3. Know what to do when an error genuinely cannot be priced.

---

## 📚 Theory

### Two errors, never equal

Every model that says yes or no makes exactly two kinds of mistake, and they land on different people.

- **A miss** — fraud goes through. The money leaves. Risk feels it.
- **A false alarm** — an honest customer is blocked. Support feels it, and so does the customer.

A single accuracy number adds these two together as if they were the same event, which is the trap module P1 opened with. Everything useful starts when you separate them and price each one.

### Put a number on both, even a rough one

Product managers hesitate here because the numbers feel unreliable. They are unreliable. They are still better than the alternative, which is deciding by whoever argued hardest.

A workable estimate for each side is usually available:

- **A miss:** the average fraudulent amount, plus the chargeback fee. Finance has this.
- **A false alarm:** the cost of a support contact, plus some allowance for the customers who leave. Support has the first part; the second is a judgement you write down and label as a judgement.

Order of magnitude is enough. When one error is forty times the other, you do not need the second decimal place — you need to know which way the asymmetry runs.

### Capacity is the constraint people forget

Most flagged transactions are reviewed by a human before anything happens. That team has a size. If they can handle 500 alerts a day, a threshold producing 900 does not catch more fraud — it produces a backlog, and the oldest alerts age past usefulness.

So the threshold is usually set by two things at once: the cost ratio, and the number of cases the organisation can actually act on. A model handed to a team with no capacity to act is a feature that changes nothing.

### When an error cannot be priced

Some errors resist a number: a safety incident, a discriminatory outcome, a story in the press. Do not invent a figure to make the arithmetic close. Say plainly that this error is not being traded against money, and set a floor instead — *"we will not ship above this false-block rate for this customer segment, whatever it does to fraud losses."*

That is a policy decision rather than an optimisation, and writing it down as one is what keeps it from being quietly optimised away later. Module P18 goes further into this.

## 💻 Practice

Here are the numbers on the table.

> The wallet sees about **60 fraudulent transactions a day**. The review team can handle **500 alerts a day**, and each alert costs about **$2** of an analyst's time. A missed fraud costs on average **$220**. A false block costs about **$5** in support and lost goodwill.
>
> **Threshold A:** 300 alerts a day, catches 40% of the fraud.
> **Threshold B:** 500 alerts a day, catches 62% of the fraud.

Which threshold, and what do you tell the two people in the room?

<details>
<summary><b>Show a model answer</b></summary>

**Threshold A.** Caught: 40% of 60 = 24 fraud a day, worth 24 × $220 = **$5,280**. Against it: 276 false alarms × $5 = $1,380, and 300 reviews × $2 = $600. **Net ≈ $3,300 a day.**

**Threshold B.** Caught: 62% of 60 = 37 a day, worth 37 × $220 = **$8,140**. Against it: 463 false alarms × $5 = $2,315, and 500 reviews × $2 = $1,000. **Net ≈ $4,825 a day.**

**Take B** — about $1,500 a day better, roughly half a million a year. And B is at the capacity ceiling, so it is also the most the current team can support: going lower would build a backlog rather than catch more fraud.

**To Risk:** "We are catching 62%, which is the most this review team can process. If you want more, the next step is not a threshold change, it is more reviewers — and now we know what each additional one is worth."

**To Support:** "463 of the 500 daily alerts will be honest customers. That is deliberate: a missed fraud costs forty-four times what a false alarm does. What I owe you is that the block is quick to appeal and never silent — and that is a requirement on the feature, not a nice-to-have."

Notice the shape of the answer. The arithmetic decided the threshold; the conversation with Support turned into a product requirement; and Risk got a costed answer about hiring instead of a refusal.
</details>

## 📌 Remember

- A yes/no model makes exactly two kinds of mistake, they land on different people, and they are almost never equally expensive.
- Price both, roughly. An order of magnitude beats deciding by whoever argues hardest.
- The threshold does not improve the model. It chooses which mistake you prefer, and how often you make it.
- Capacity is part of the decision: alerts nobody can act on are a backlog, not protection.
- When an error cannot honestly be priced, set a floor and call it a policy. Do not invent a number to make the arithmetic close.
