# Module P23. The ML product strategy

> **Level:** Advanced · **Time:** ~60 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

Viktor asks for one page.

> "The board wants to know what our ML strategy is. Not the project list — they have that. One page on what we are building towards, and why it is worth having rather than buying."

It is the hardest document in the track, because everything that made the previous twenty-two modules tractable is absent. There is no decision to specify, no metric chain, no threshold. And the honest first draft of most such pages is a list of models, which answers a question nobody asked.

## 🎯 Your task

1. Say where the advantage comes from, and be right about it.
2. Decide when a shared platform is worth building.
3. Write a page a board can act on, containing no model names.

Three questions establish whether you have one:

1. **What data do we have that a competitor cannot get?** Not "a lot of data" — data that arises only from running this particular business.
2. **What loop makes it grow without anyone deciding to grow it?** Module P17's virtuous version: every use produces a label.
3. **What does it let us decide better than they can?** If the answer is nothing, this is a data lake and not an advantage.

### Decisions first, capability second

The failure mode of ML strategy is a capability nobody is waiting for: a feature store with no second consumer, a forecasting platform and no one whose plan changes because of a forecast. Every strategy item should be traceable to a decision somebody makes, with a cost of getting it wrong.

Start from the decision, and let the capability be justified by it. Reversed, you get a sophisticated cost centre that takes two years to admit.

### Platform: the rule of three

Do not build shared infrastructure for the first feature, or the second. Build it when **the third one needs the same thing** — because at that point you have seen enough variation to build something that fits, and you also have three customers, which is what makes it a platform rather than an abstraction built on one example.

In this track, the third feature justified four things: the eval harness from module P21, a point-in-time feature layer so that module P4's trap is structurally hard to fall into, serving with flags and logging from module P13, and the hold-out and labelling machinery from module P17. Each of those was built for one feature and is now used by three, which is the only honest reason to call any of it a platform.

### What a twelve-month plan contains

Four sections and a fifth that most people omit:

1. **Three or four decisions we will make better**, each with money attached.
2. **The platform investments the third feature justified** — and no others.
3. **The capability bets, with decision dates.** Module P19's portfolio.
4. **What we will stop**, and why.
5. **What we will not do, and why** — the section that makes the page credible, because a strategy that excludes nothing has not chosen anything.

### How to talk to a board about this

No model names, no architectures, no accuracy figures. Three things they can act on: the decisions that get better, the money, and the dates on which you will know the rest. If a sentence cannot be followed by "so what?", it does not belong on the page.

### Datacore — how we use machine learning, and why we own it

**Where our advantage comes from.** We see the full path of an order: what was searched for, what was bought, which warehouse shipped it, which courier carried it, whether it arrived late, whether it was refunded, and whether the customer came back. Nobody outside this company can assemble that, and no model can be bought that contains it. Every decision we automate adds to it, because we now record the outcome of each one. That loop, not any particular model, is the asset.

**Four decisions we already make better, and what they are worth.**

| Decision | Annual value |
| --- | --- |
| Which refund requests are approved without a person | **$2.2M** |
| Which seller photos reach the catalogue | **$1.4M** |
| Which support replies a person has to write from scratch | **$0.78M** |
| Where the fraud threshold sits, and who scores the transaction | **$0.4M** |
| *The delivery advisory, for completeness* | *$14K* |
| **Total** | **≈ $4.8M** |

The advisory is on this page deliberately. It is a rule, it earns fourteen thousand dollars a year, and we are not spending another quarter on it. A strategy that cannot name its smallest result is hiding something.

**What we will build as shared capability, and why now.** Four things, each already used by three features — which is the test, and the reason none of them were built in year one:

- The eval harness. Any change of prompt, model or vendor is now a two-hour question instead of a two-week argument.
- A point-in-time feature layer, so that a feature which did not exist at the moment of the decision is hard to use by accident. The single most expensive mistake in this field, made structurally unlikely.
- Serving with flags, logging and rollback in under a minute, for every model.
- Hold-out and labelling machinery, because the decisions we automate stop teaching us anything the moment we automate them.

**Two bets, with dates rather than promises.** Search ranking is worth about $2.4M a year and I put it at roughly 70%; we will know on **28 March**. Warehouse stocking is worth about $6M a year and I put it at roughly 30%, which is why it has two weeks and not a quarter; we will know on **14 February**.

**What we will stop.** Manual review of the 62% of refund requests the model handles — that is the $2.2M, and it is the team of 88 becoming a team that reads only the cases that need judgement. We are also stopping further work on the delivery advisory.

**What we will not do this year, and why.**

- **We will not build our own speech or translation capability.** Both are utilities; we would spend a year to arrive where an API already is.
- **We will not let a system send customer messages or deny a refund by itself.** Sending informational replies automatically is worth doing and is scoped; denials and commitments stay with a person, because the two errors are not the same kind of error and one of them takes money from someone who was entitled to it.
- **We will not move fraud scoring permanently to a vendor.** We will keep buying thirteen points of detection while our own model stays alive and the exit stays priced, and we will revisit when either changes.

**The one sentence, if the board reads nothing else.** *Our machine learning is worth about $4.8M a year today, all of it from decisions we now make faster or better than a person could; the reason it is ours rather than rented is that every one of those decisions writes down its own outcome, and nobody else can buy that history.*

## 📌 Remember

- The moat is the loop, not the model. A competitor can call the same model this afternoon and cannot buy your recorded outcomes.
- Three questions: what data arises only from running this business, what loop grows it without anyone deciding to, and what it lets you decide better.
- Decisions first, capability second. A capability with no decision waiting for it is a sophisticated cost centre that takes two years to admit.
- Build platform at the third feature that needs the same thing — that is when you have both enough variation to fit and enough customers to call it one.
- A twelve-month plan has five sections, and the one people omit is what you will not do. A strategy that excludes nothing has chosen nothing.
- Name your smallest result. A page that reports only the wins is read as marketing, and correctly.
- To a board: the decisions that get better, the money, and the dates you will know the rest. No model names, no accuracy figures.
