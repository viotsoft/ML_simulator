# Module P9. Designing for a model that is wrong

> **Level:** ML PM · **Time:** ~50 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

Design review for the photo moderation feature. Sellers upload product photos; the model flags ones that do not match the chosen category. The mock on screen is a single screen with a single outcome:

> **Photo rejected.** This image does not match the selected category.

The designer is pleased with it. Then the support lead asks the question that ends the review:

> "The model is wrong about one photo in twelve. What does the seller do on those? Because right now they read a sentence that says they are wrong, from a system with no name and no argument, and then they call me."

Igor adds the part that makes it your problem rather than the designer's:

> "I can give you a confidence number with every prediction. What you do with it is a product decision. If you ignore it, you are choosing to treat a 0.51 exactly like a 0.99."

## 🎯 Your task

1. Turn one confidence number into distinct product behaviours.
2. Write copy that states a rate rather than a certainty.
3. Make a wrong prediction cost the user a click rather than a sale.

---

## 📚 Theory

### One number, three behaviours

Every useful model returns not only an answer but a confidence. A feature that ignores that number throws away the most valuable thing the model produced: its own admission of doubt.

The pattern is three bands with three different behaviours.

The silent band is the one product managers skip and the one that saves them. Showing nothing is a legitimate product behaviour: the user gets the pre-model experience, which was acceptable last quarter and is still acceptable now.

### Say a rate, not a certainty

"Your order will be late" is a claim the product cannot support, and being wrong about it costs trust in everything else on the page. "Orders like this arrive late about 1 time in 4" is both true and more useful, because the user can act on it with their own judgement.

The rule generalises: describe the population, not the individual. "Photos like this are usually rejected on review" survives being wrong. "This photo violates the rules" does not.

### A human in the loop needs two things

Routing the middle band to a reviewer only helps if the reviewer can act. That takes:

- **The why.** Not the confidence score — the evidence. *"Category says 'headphones'; the image looks like a phone case."* A reviewer given only a number ends up rubber-stamping, and you have built an expensive random number generator.
- **A cheap way to disagree.** One click, no form, no justification. If disagreeing is expensive, the queue silently converges on whatever the model said, and you lose the human without noticing.

### Failing gracefully

The design question is not "how do we avoid being wrong" — you will be wrong at a known rate. It is *what does being wrong cost the person on the other side.*

- A wrong warning that costs a click: acceptable.
- A wrong rejection that costs a seller their listing until they find support's phone number: not acceptable, at any accuracy.

So: no silent irreversible actions. If the model's decision cannot be undone by the person it affects, it needs either a human in front of it or a reversal path that takes one click. And the user must be told that a decision was made, by what, and how to contest it — which in many jurisdictions is not a design preference but a requirement, as module P18 covers.

## 💻 Practice

Three proposals for photo moderation are on the table. Pick one and defend it.

> **A.** Reject automatically above 0.5. Copy: "Photo rejected. This image does not match the selected category."
> **B.** Reject automatically above 0.9. Between 0.6 and 0.9, send to human review. Below 0.6, accept.
> **C.** Never reject automatically. Every flagged photo goes to human review.

The moderation team handles about 400 items a day. The platform receives 9,000 photos a day, and roughly 6% genuinely violate the category rules.

<details>
<summary><b>Show a model answer</b></summary>

**B, with three additions.**

**Why not C.** It looks like the safe answer and it fails on arithmetic. Flagging even 10% of 9,000 photos is 900 items a day against a capacity of 400 — the queue grows by 500 a day forever, and sellers wait a week to publish. Module P6's capacity constraint is a design constraint here, not a footnote.

**Why not A.** A single threshold at 0.5 means an irreversible action taken on the model's least reliable predictions, delivered as a verdict, with no path back. This is the design that generates the support calls the review opened with — and the copy states a certainty the model does not have.

**B, sized to capacity.** The upper band automates the clear cases. The middle band is set to produce **about 350 items a day**, which is what the team can actually clear — the thresholds follow from the capacity, not the other way round. Below the lower threshold, accept and say nothing.

**Addition 1 — the copy.** Automatic rejection reads: *"We could not match this photo to 'Headphones'. Photos like this are usually in the wrong category. Change the category, replace the photo, or ask us to look again."* It names the reason, states a rate, and offers three actions, one of which is a human.

**Addition 2 — one-click appeal with an answer.** The appeal goes into the same moderation queue with a stated response time. An appeal path with no SLA is decoration.

**Addition 3 — the reviewer's screen.** The photo, the chosen category, and the model's evidence in one line. No confidence score on its own: it produces rubber-stamping, and a rubber-stamped queue is worse than automation, because it costs money and provides the same answer.

**What you tell the designer.** The single-screen mock is not wrong, it is one of three screens. The other two — the quiet one and the human one — are where the 1-in-12 lives.
</details>

## 📌 Remember

- A model returns a confidence. A feature that ignores it treats 0.51 exactly like 0.99.
- Three bands, three behaviours: act, ask a human, stay silent. Silence is a legitimate product behaviour — it returns the user to the experience that was acceptable before the model.
- Size the human band by the capacity of the people in it. Thresholds follow from capacity as much as from the cost of errors.
- State a rate about a population, not a certainty about the individual. A rate survives being wrong.
- A reviewer needs the evidence and a cheap way to disagree; given only a score, they will rubber-stamp.
- No silent irreversible actions. If the affected person cannot undo it, either a human goes in front of it or the reversal is one click.
