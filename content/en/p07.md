# Module P7. Sizing an ML project

> **Level:** ML PM · **Time:** ~50 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

Quarterly planning. Viktor, the CTO, goes down the list and stops at the delay warning.

> "When is it in the app?"

Lena, the ML lead, answers honestly:

> "Six weeks if the signal is in the data. Possibly never — we will not know until we look."

Viktor turns to you.

> "I cannot take 'possibly never' to the board. They are approving a budget. Give me a date."

Both of them are right, which is why this lands on you. Lena cannot promise an accuracy she has not measured. Viktor cannot fund an open-ended search. Your job is to produce something that is honest about the uncertainty and still plannable.

## 🎯 Your task

1. Split the project into parts whose uncertainty is genuinely different.
2. Put the uncertain part in a box with a budget and a stopping rule.
3. Commit to decision dates rather than to accuracy.

---

## 📚 Theory

### You are estimating a search, not a build

On an ordinary feature the work is known and the risk is in execution: the API might be slower than documented, the designer might be on holiday. On an ML feature the central question — *is there enough signal in this data to change a decision* — cannot be answered without doing part of the work.

Nobody is withholding that answer from you. It does not exist yet. An estimate that ignores this is not an estimate, it is a promise somebody will be held to.

### Most of the project is not uncertain

The useful move is to stop estimating "the model" as one thing. A typical project has four stages and only one of them is unbounded.

Note the last stage. Serving the model, monitoring it, building the fallback and the appeal path is frequently *larger* than the modelling, and it is the part that gets forgotten in the estimate — because it is invisible in the notebook where the model was born.

### Put the search in a box

A time-boxed spike has three properties: a fixed duration, a question, and a decision at the end. Not "build the delay model" but:

> **By 14 October**, tell us whether a model beats the shipped rule by more than 3 points of macro-F1 at the same alert volume.

The output of a spike is a decision, not a feature. That is what makes it safe to fund: Viktor is not buying a model, he is buying an answer by a date, and he knows the price of the answer in advance.

### The stopping criterion belongs in the brief

Write it before the work starts, while nobody is attached to the result:

> If after three weeks the model does not beat the rule by 3 points, we ship the rule and close the project.

Written afterwards, it stops being a criterion and becomes a negotiation — and the negotiation always ends in two more weeks, every two weeks. Module P10 is entirely about this gate.

### What you actually commit to

Two kinds of date, and only one of them is safe to give.

- **Decision dates** — *"on 14 October I will tell you whether this works."* Always committable. The date does not depend on the result.
- **Delivery dates** — committable for everything that does not depend on the search: the rule, the interface, the logging, the metric chain.

Viktor can plan a board conversation around a decision date. He can plan nothing at all around a promise of accuracy, which is why asking the team for one is worse than useless: it gets you a number chosen to be acceptable rather than true.

## 💻 Practice

Turn "six weeks if the signal is there, possibly never" into something you can say out loud in the planning meeting.

<details>
<summary><b>Show a model answer</b></summary>

**The plan.**

| When | What | What comes out of it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Week 1 | Data audit — module P4's four questions | A memo. Decision: does the project continue at all |
| Week 2 | Ship the rule: two or more items from the far warehouse, plus a weekend → warning | The decision, the interface, the logging and the metric chain are live. And a baseline number |
| Weeks 3–5 | Boxed modelling spike | **14 October:** iterate once more, productionise, or stop |
| Weeks 6–7 | Productionisation, only if the model won | The model behind the same interface |

**What you say to Viktor.**

> "The warning is in the app in two weeks. It will be a rule, and it may turn out to be all we need — we will know, because it is instrumented. On 14 October I will tell you whether a model is worth another month, with the number it has to beat written down today. If the answer is no, we keep the rule, and the whole search will have cost three weeks."

Three things make this plannable. The user-visible change has a real date, because it does not depend on the search. The search has a cap, so the downside is three weeks rather than a quarter. And the meeting on 14 October has an agenda: one number against one threshold.

**What not to say.** "Six weeks." You would be inventing the part Lena honestly said she does not know, and you would own it in November.
</details>

## 📌 Remember

- An ML estimate is an estimate of a search. The central question cannot be answered without doing part of the work.
- Split the project: the audit, the baseline and the productionisation estimate like ordinary engineering. Only the modelling is unbounded.
- Productionisation is routinely the largest stage and the one left out of the estimate.
- Box the search: a fixed duration, a question, and a decision at the end. A spike delivers a decision, not a feature.
- Write the stopping criterion before the work starts, or it becomes a negotiation that ends in two more weeks, every two weeks.
- Commit to decision dates. Commit to delivery dates only for the parts that do not depend on the search.
