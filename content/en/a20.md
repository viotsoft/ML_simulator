# Module A20. Capstone: defending the platform

> **Level:** Senior AI · **Time:** ~90 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

A year in. The agentic marketing factory works: outbound, inbound triage, content. Marina calls a meeting and says something you did not expect:

> "Three customers have asked how we did this. Two would pay if we sold them the same thing. So the question on the table: do we turn an internal tool into a product?
>
> I need two documents from you. First, an architecture review for our CTO: will this survive someone else's company. Second, a go-to-market plan: how we package it, what we charge, and how we get through their security people."

This is the track's final task: fold everything you built into two pages that different people read with different questions.

## 🎯 Your task

1. Run an honest architecture review of your system — including what breaks on someone else's data.
2. Build the go-to-market plan: packaging, price, pilot, procurement.
3. Defend both documents before the CTO and the commercial team.

### The architecture review: the CTO's nine questions

A good review answers them before they are asked:

1. **What is the system?** A one-screen diagram: data flows, trust boundaries, where secrets live.
2. **Where are decisions made?** What code decides, what the model decides, where the human gates are (A11).
3. **What happens when each dependency fails?** Provider, CRM, knowledge base, queue (A13).
4. **How are customers isolated?** Data, memory, knowledge base, traces, keys.
5. **What do we do about injection and exfiltration?** The layers from A15 — and honestly, which one is thin.
6. **How do we learn that quality dropped?** Metrics, canary, stop rules (A6, A16).
7. **What does it cost and how does it scale?** Cost per unit, bottlenecks (A12).
8. **What do we deliberately not do?** Explicit limits are a strong part of a review, not a weak one.
9. **What is the most likely failure scenario?** The one you name yourself is usually the one that happens.

The defense rule: **answer question nine first**. A review where the engineer names the main risk goes faster than one where the CTO has to find it.

### Packaging and price

Three pricing models for an agentic product:

| Model | When it works | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Per seat | The user works with the agent daily | The customer economizes by buying fewer seats |
| Per run / volume | Value scales directly with volume | The customer fears an unpredictable bill |
| Per outcome (meeting, resolved ticket) | The outcome is measurable and you control it | Attribution disputes: whose outcome is it |

For first sales: **a platform fee plus metered volume with a cap**. The cap removes the buyer's main fear — "what if it generates ten thousand dollars' worth".

And separately on cost: from A19 you know your cost per unit. The price must cover it with room for support — agentic products carry lower margins than classic SaaS, because every run costs money.

### Pilot and procurement

A pilot that ends in a purchase is built like this:

- **Duration:** 4–6 weeks. Shorter gives no statistics, longer loses the sponsor.
- **The success criterion is written down in advance** and signed by the customer: "80% of drafts without edits and zero emails containing invented facts".
- **On the customer's data, not a demo.** That is the only way to learn that your RAG cannot digest their documentation.
- **A named owner on the customer's side.** A pilot without one ends in nothing.

Procurement is separate work most engineers are unprepared for. You will be asked: where the data is stored and in which jurisdiction, which of your employees has access, what happens to data on termination, whether you have a DPA and who the sub-processors are (the model provider is a sub-processor!), whether models train on their data, how tenants are isolated, whether you have SOC 2 or a plan for it.

Prepare these answers **before** the first sale: a 200-item security questionnaire arrives when the deal is already in flight and delays it by months.

## 📌 Remember

- A product differs from an internal tool by tenant isolation, configurability and SLA — that is half a new build, not polish.
- In an architecture review, name the main risk first: then it gets discussed rather than hunted.
- "What the system does not do" is a strong section: explicit limits show you understand the system.
- First sales suit a platform fee plus capped metered volume: the cap removes the fear of an unpredictable bill.
- Agentic products carry lower margins than classic SaaS: every run costs money and the price must reflect it.
- A pilot works under three conditions: the customer's data, a success criterion agreed up front, and a named owner on their side.
- Prepare security-questionnaire answers before the first deal, or they will delay it by months.

**Further reading:** multi-tenant SaaS architecture material; standard security questionnaires (SIG Lite, CAIQ) — read one end to end in advance; books on enterprise sales of technical products; and your whole A1–A19 track — that is the content of both documents.

## 🎓 End of the track

Twenty modules ago a CEO said "let's build a marketing factory" and you did not know where to start. Now you have a system running in production and two documents you could sell it with.

What you can do now that most people cannot: frame an agentic problem from the unit of work and the cost of a mistake, build pipelines with checks and a human in the loop, measure the quality of a non-deterministic system, keep cost under control, survive outages and attacks, ship without surprises, and discuss your system in the language of a CFO and a procurement officer.

Classic ML was the first track. Together they make a rare combination: an engineer who can both train a model and take an agentic system all the way to revenue.
