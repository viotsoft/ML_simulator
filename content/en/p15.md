# Module P15. Unit economics of an ML feature

> **Level:** Senior ML PM · **Time:** ~55 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

Marina from finance sends you one line before the quarterly budget review.

> "What does photo moderation cost us per photo?"

You open the cloud bill and find a single line item called **ML platform, $14,200**. It covers four models, three teams and a training cluster. Nobody can split it, and nobody has ever needed to.

Igor is enthusiastic about a new vision model that is four points better on the benchmark. Marina wants to know the per-photo number before she hears about benchmarks. She is right to: a feature whose cost is only known as a monthly lump sum cannot be compared to anything, cannot be forecast when volume triples, and cannot be defended.

## 🎯 Your task

1. Break an ML feature's cost into the four lines it actually has.
2. Work in cost per decision rather than cost per month.
3. Find out which line dominates before arguing about any of them.

---

## 📚 Theory

### Four cost lines

- **Inference.** Per prediction. The only one most people think of, and often the smallest.
- **Human review.** Every case routed to the middle band from module P9, at a real hourly rate. This is frequently the largest line, and it scales linearly with volume in the worst way.
- **Labelling and data.** Annotation, storage, the pipelines that must not break.
- **People.** Retraining, monitoring, the on-call rotation, the engineer-days. This is the line from module P10 that never appears on a cloud bill.

And a fifth that is not a cost but belongs in the same table: **the cost of the errors you still make.** A model that misses a violation has not saved you money by being cheap.

### Cost per decision, not per month

A monthly figure cannot be compared to anything. Cost *per decision* can be set against the value per decision from module P6, survives a change in volume, and makes two architectures comparable in one line. It is also the number that reveals the trap in the next section.

### The expensive-model trap, and the cascade

A model that is four points better and twenty-five times more expensive per call is not four points better as a product. At scale, that multiplier meets your volume and becomes the dominant cost line.

The structure that usually beats both options is a **cascade**: the cheap model runs on everything, and the expensive one is called only on the cases the cheap model is unsure about. Module P9's three bands are already the right shape for this — the middle band does not have to go to a human, it can go to a better model first.

### Labelling is a product decision

How many labels, produced by whom, and at what quality is a budget question you own. The cheapest labels are the ones the product generates as a side effect — a moderator's decision, a customer's appeal, a refund — which is why module P17 treats the feedback loop as an asset. Paying per item for annotation is sometimes right, and it should be a deliberate purchase with a number attached, not a surprise on somebody's credit card.

### What scales badly

Two lines grow faster than the business and deserve a forecast at 10× volume before launch: **per-call model pricing**, and **human review**. A feature that is comfortable at 9,000 items a day and impossible at 90,000 is not a strategy, and the time to find that out is now.

## 💻 Practice

Three options for photo moderation. Which one, and what do you tell Marina?

> **Volume:** 9,000 photos a day, about 270,000 a month. **6%** genuinely violate the category rules, so roughly **16,200 violations a month**. A violation that reaches the catalogue costs about **$9** in complaints, returns and lost trust. Human review costs **$0.90** an item, and the team can handle up to 12,000 a month.
>
> **A — cheap classifier.** $0.0004 a photo. Catches 78% of violations. Sends 4% of photos to review.
> **B — large vision model API.** $0.04 a photo. Catches 91%. Sends 3% to review.
> **C — cascade.** The cheap model on everything, the large model on the 15% it is unsure about: $0.0064 a photo. Catches 89%. Sends 3.5% to review.

**One table, three lines each.**

| | Inference | Review | Violations missed | **Total / month** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A — cheap | $108 | 10,800 × $0.90 = $9,720 | 3,564 × $9 = $32,076 | **$41,904** |
| B — expensive | $10,800 | 8,100 × $0.90 = $7,290 | 1,458 × $9 = $13,122 | **$31,212** |
| C — cascade | $1,728 | 9,450 × $0.90 = $8,505 | 1,782 × $9 = $16,038 | **$26,271** |

**Take C.** It delivers 89 of B's 91 points of catch rate for 16% of B's inference bill, and beats it by about **$4,900 a month**. All three fit inside the review team's 12,000-item capacity, so capacity is not the binding constraint here — worth stating explicitly, because in module P9's version of this problem it was.

**Cost per decision, since that was the question.** A: $0.155. B: $0.116. C: **$0.097** per photo, all in. That is the number Marina asked for, and it is the one that survives volume tripling.

**The finding that matters more than the choice.** In every option the largest block is the violations that still get through — $13,000 to $32,000 a month against an inference line of $108 to $10,800. The leverage is in the catch rate, not in the price per call. Igor's four-points-better model is interesting for exactly this reason, and the cheap-versus-expensive argument was an argument about the smallest number on the page.

**What you forecast before committing.** At 90,000 photos a day, B's inference alone is $108,000 a month and review is over capacity for every option. C's advantage grows to roughly $49,000 a month. A decision that is close today is not close at 10× volume, in C's favour.

**What you say to Marina.** *"$0.097 per photo, and of that, nine tenths is not the model — it is the cost of the violations we still miss. If you want the number down, fund catch rate, not cheaper inference."*
</details>

## 📌 Remember

- Four cost lines: inference, human review, labelling and data, and people. Inference is the one everyone names and often the smallest.
- Add the cost of the errors you still make. A cheap model that misses more has not saved anything.
- Work in cost per decision. A monthly lump sum cannot be compared, forecast, or defended.
- A model that is slightly better and much more expensive per call meets your volume and becomes the dominant line. A cascade — cheap model everywhere, expensive model only on the uncertain band — usually beats both.
- Find the dominant line before arguing. Optimising the smallest number on the page is the most common way to spend a quarter on nothing.
- Per-call pricing and human review scale badly. Forecast both at 10× volume before you commit.
