# Module P12. The experiment

> **Level:** ML PM · **Time:** ~50 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

Three weeks of A/B on the delay advisory. The results land, and the room splits immediately.

The designer reads the top line:

> "Slot changes up 6%. People are using it. Ship it."

The analyst reads the bottom line:

> "Checkout conversion is down 0.4 points. That is revenue."

Both numbers are real. Both are outside their confidence intervals. And they point in opposite directions, which is the most informative thing an experiment can do and the least comfortable.

Somebody has to say what the experiment means. That is you, and the answer is not a compromise.

## 🎯 Your task

1. Design the test so that its answer is about the feature and not about noise.
2. Know which metric wins when two metrics disagree, and why.
3. Turn a failed experiment into the next version rather than into an argument.

---

## 📚 Theory

### Randomise people, not predictions

The unit of randomisation is the **customer**, for the whole duration. Randomise by session and the same person sees the advisory on Tuesday and not on Thursday; randomise per prediction and the two groups stop being groups. Both leaks contaminate the comparison in the direction of "no effect", which is the worst kind of wrong: it looks like a clean negative result.

### You are testing the feature, not the model

The A group gets the current experience; the B group gets the model *plus the threshold plus the copy plus the placement*. If B loses, you have not learned that the model is bad — you have learned that this bundle is worse. Which part is at fault is the next question, and it is usually not the model.

### Declare the deciding number and the guardrails before you start

From module P5: one primary metric that decides, plus guardrails that must not get worse. Pre-registration is not bureaucracy — it is the only thing standing between you and a review where six metrics are on the slide and somebody picks the friendliest one.

Also decide the runtime in advance and then leave it alone. Stopping the moment a number looks good is how noise gets promoted to a launch, and a feature's first days are distorted anyway: people react to anything new, and that reaction fades.

This is why you write the chain down as a claim in module P5. When the proxy moves and the business metric does not follow, something real has been learned: engagement with this feature is not the same thing as value from it. People clicking the slot-change button may be people who were about to buy and are now reconsidering.

### A guardrail breach outranks a hit on the primary metric

If the deciding number is met and a guardrail is broken, the answer is not "ship, the main metric won". The guardrail exists precisely for a win that is hiding damage, and its whole value is that it was agreed before anyone had a result to defend.

## 💻 Practice

Here is the full readout. Ship, iterate, or stop?

> **Setup.** Randomised by customer, 3 weeks, 50/50. 1.2M orders a month, average order $34 — about $40.8M of monthly revenue. Late-delivery complaints cost about $6 each and run at 3,600 a month.
>
> **Slot changes among advised customers:** +6.2% (±0.9).
> **Late-delivery complaints:** −9.1% (±1.8). *The PRD's deciding number was −8%.*
> **Checkout conversion:** 19.4% → 19.0%, i.e. −0.4pp (±0.12). *The guardrail was "no worse than −0.3pp".*
> **Revenue per visitor:** −2.1%.

<details>
<summary><b>Show a model answer</b></summary>

**Put both sides in the same currency first.**

- Complaints avoided: 9.1% of 3,600 × $6 ≈ **$1,966 a month.**
- Revenue lost: 2.1% of $40.8M ≈ **$857,000 a month.**

The feature is buying two thousand dollars of support relief for eight hundred and fifty thousand dollars of revenue. It works exactly as designed, hits its agreed deciding number, and would cost roughly **$10M a year.**

**Decision: do not ship. And do not treat this as a failure of the model.**

**What actually happened.** The advisory appears *before payment*. Told that their order might be late, a small share of customers stop and think, and thinking at checkout costs conversions. The model was right, the copy was honest, and the placement was wrong. This is module P3's fourth slot — the moment — deciding the outcome of the whole project.

**Version two: move the moment.**

> Show the advisory on the confirmation screen and in the order email, *after* payment, together with a free slot change.

The decision it enables is unchanged — the customer can still reschedule before dispatch — but the purchase is no longer at risk, because it has already happened. Expect a smaller complaint reduction, because some customers will not read the email. A smaller reduction against no revenue cost is a far better trade than the one we just measured.

Two changes worth testing alongside it: a higher threshold, so the advisory appears only on the orders most likely to be late; and copy framed as a choice rather than a warning — *"pick a guaranteed evening slot"* rather than *"your order may be late"*.

**What this experiment was worth.** The guardrail did its job. Without it, the launch memo would have read "complaints down 9%, deciding number exceeded", the feature would have shipped, and the $10M would have shown up months later as an unexplained conversion dip that nobody connected to a delivery advisory. The uncomfortable result is the one that paid for the experiment.

**And what you say to the designer.** Slot changes did go up 6%, and that number is real. It just turned out to measure hesitation, not helpfulness.
</details>

## 📌 Remember

- Randomise by customer for the whole test. Randomising by session or per prediction contaminates the comparison towards "no effect" — a false clean negative.
- You are testing the bundle: model, threshold, copy and placement. When B loses, the model is usually not the part at fault.
- Pre-register the deciding number, the guardrails and the runtime. Stopping early promotes noise, and the first days are distorted by novelty anyway.
- When the proxy rises and the business metric falls, the proxy is refuted. It only ever existed as a hypothesis about the metric that matters.
- A broken guardrail outranks a hit on the primary metric. That is the situation it was written for.
- A negative result that identifies *which* part was wrong is a specification for version two.
