# Module P5. Metrics that survive a product review

> **Level:** PM Foundations · **Time:** ~45 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

Quarterly review. Victor is there, Max is there, and so is someone from finance who has never attended one of these before and is here because the ML team's budget is being discussed.

Lena's slide says:

> **macro-F1: 0.86 → 0.91**

Victor nods. Finance asks the question that ends the meeting:

> "And what does that mean for us?"

Nobody has a sentence ready. Lena can explain exactly what macro-F1 measures and does. It takes ninety seconds and it does not answer the question. The feature is deferred to next quarter "pending clarification", which is how a good project dies.

The problem was not the metric. The problem was that it was the only one on the slide.

## 🎯 Your task

1. Build the chain from a business number to a model number and back.
2. Know which metric answers which audience, and which one decides.
3. Add guardrails so that winning on one number cannot mean losing overall.

---

## 📚 Theory

### Three metrics, three jobs

An ML feature needs all three. They are not competing candidates; they answer different questions and they are read by different people.

**The business metric.** Money, or something the company already tracks and cares about: margin, retention, support cost, hours saved. This is the number that decides whether the feature was worth building. It is also the slowest to move and the noisiest.

**The online proxy.** What you measure in the experiment: the metric that moves within a couple of weeks and is plausibly upstream of the business metric. Orders per session, tickets resolved without a human, share of deliveries promised accurately. This is what the A/B actually reads — module P12 is about designing that test.

**The offline metric.** What the team optimises between releases: F1, WAPE, recall at a fixed alert budget. It is a filter for candidates, nothing more. It is also the only one available before anything ships, which is exactly why teams mistake it for the verdict.

### The chain is a claim, not a fact

"macro-F1 up five points" → "more tickets land in the right queue" → "less time per ticket, lower support cost". Each arrow is a hypothesis. Sometimes they hold and sometimes the middle one quietly fails: tickets land in the right queue and agents still re-read everything, so nothing is saved.

Write the chain down anyway, precisely because it can fail. A written chain is a claim you can test; an unwritten one is an assumption nobody notices until the feature has shipped and the business metric has not moved.

### One number decides

Reviews go wrong when three metrics improve, one worsens, and everyone argues about which matters. Decide **before** the experiment which single number decides go or no-go. Everything else is context.

For that number, prefer the one the company already uses. A metric invented for this project has to be defended before it can be used, and it will be — usually at the worst moment.

### Guardrails

A guardrail is a metric you are not trying to improve and refuse to damage: overall conversion, page load time, refund rate, support volume. It exists because almost any single metric can be won by breaking something else. A recommendation block can lift clicks by pushing cheap, clickable items and cost you revenue — the ML track's system design module has that exact example.

Name two or three, and name them before the test, not while reading the results.

### Why accuracy is not a KPI

It is not that accuracy is a bad metric. It is that no business tracks it, no decision is taken on it, and it hides which errors were made — module P1 pulls that apart. A metric belongs on a review slide when someone in the room acts differently depending on its value. That is the test.

## 💻 Practice

Build the metric chain for the delivery promise: at checkout, warn the customer when an order is likely to be late.

Give the business metric, the online proxy, the offline metric, two guardrails, and the one number that decides.

<details>
<summary><b>Show a model answer</b></summary>

**Business metric:** support contacts about "where is my order", plus the refund and goodwill cost of late deliveries. Both are already tracked, which is what makes them usable.

**Online proxy:** share of orders where the promise matched reality, measured over a two-week A/B. It moves quickly and sits plausibly upstream of the business metric.

**Offline metric:** recall on late orders at a fixed rate of false warnings — because the team needs something to optimise between releases, and it must reflect that the two errors differ (module P6).

**Guardrails:** checkout conversion, and average items per order. A pessimistic promise is the easy way to look accurate while quietly scaring people out of buying. That is the failure this feature is most likely to produce, so it gets a guardrail.

**The deciding number:** "where is my order" contacts per thousand orders. It is the business metric, the support team already reports it weekly, and nobody has to be persuaded that it matters.

Notice what the chain buys you. When finance asks "and what does that mean", the answer is one sentence: *we expect fewer people asking where their order is, and we will know in two weeks.*
</details>

## 📌 Remember

- Three metrics with three jobs: business decides worth, the online proxy moves in the experiment, the offline metric filters candidates between releases.
- The chain between them is a claim. Write it down so it can be tested rather than assumed.
- Choose the one number that decides before the experiment starts, and prefer a metric the company already uses.
- Guardrails are the metrics you refuse to damage. Almost any single metric can be won by breaking another.
- A metric earns a place on the slide only if someone in the room would act differently depending on its value.
