# Module P10. Baselines and the "is it worth it" gate

> **Level:** ML PM · **Time:** ~50 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

End of the boxed spike from module P7. Igor puts up one slide.

> "Macro-F1 0.91 on the held-out month."

Viktor looks at it for a while and asks the only question that matters:

> "Is that good?"

Nobody in the room can answer, because nobody put the other numbers on the slide. The rule you shipped in week 2 has been running for three weeks and has a score. The dispatchers who currently guess at this have a score. Doing nothing has a score. Without them, 0.91 is a number with no units.

This is the meeting the whole project was aimed at, and it is yours to run.

## 🎯 Your task

1. Know which baselines to demand, and why one is never enough.
2. Set the margin worth paying for before you see the number.
3. Say no when the increment does not pay for the machinery — and write it up so the team is not punished for it.

---

## 📚 Theory

### Four baselines, and you need at least two

- **Do nothing.** The current product behaviour, scored on the same data. Often embarrassingly strong: predict "on time" for everything and you score well whenever most deliveries are on time.
- **The rule.** The heuristic you shipped in week 2. This is the honest competitor, because it already exists, already costs nothing extra, and already delivers part of the value.
- **A human.** What a dispatcher achieves on the same cases. This sets the ceiling worth aiming at and tells you whether the task is even doable.
- **Yesterday's value.** For anything forecast-shaped: repeat the last observation. Notoriously hard to beat, and notoriously absent from the slide.

### "Is it good" is unanswerable; "is the increment worth the price" is not

Reframe the meeting. The model is not competing against perfection, it is competing against the thing already running. What is on trial is **the increment**: 0.84 → 0.91, and what that difference is worth in the business metric from module P5.

### The margin is set before the number arrives

In week 2 you wrote the spike's criterion: beat the rule by 3 points. That was the *necessary* condition, and the model cleared it. The *sufficient* condition is money, and it has a second half people forget: the increment has to pay for the machinery that produces it.

A model's monthly bill is not just inference. It is retraining, monitoring, the on-call rotation that now includes a new failure mode, the data pipeline that must not break, and the engineer-days that go into all of it. Module P15 prices this properly. For the gate, a rough all-in figure is enough — and it is almost always larger than the inference line.

### Closing a project is a normal outcome

A spike that ends in "we ship the rule" is a success: it bought certainty for three weeks and freed a quarter. But that only stays true if the write-up says so. Write the memo yourself, and make it say:

- the question the spike answered, and the answer;
- the numbers, all four bars;
- what the rule now delivers in production;
- what would change the answer — the specific number that, if it moved, reopens the case.

What the memo must not do is read as a failure. A team that learns that an honest negative result is punished will bring you positive ones for the rest of your career, and you will not be able to tell which are real.

## 💻 Practice

Here is everything on the table. Ship the model, iterate again, or close the project?

> 1.2M orders a month. Late-delivery complaints run at 3 per 1,000 orders — **3,600 a month**, each costing about **$6** in support and refund handling: **$21,600 a month** of exposure.
>
> The shipped rule has cut complaints by **5.5%**. The model, from the spike's offline numbers, projects **9%**.
>
> Running the model in production: about **$1,900 a month** of inference, plus retraining, monitoring and on-call — call it **$4,000 a month** all-in.
>
> The deciding number agreed in the PRD: complaints down 8%.

<details>
<summary><b>Show a model answer</b></summary>

**The arithmetic.**

| | Complaint reduction | Value per month | Cost per month | Net |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The rule | 5.5% | $1,190 | ~0 | **+$1,190** |
| The model | 9% | $1,944 | $4,000 | **−$2,056** |

The increment the model buys is **9% − 5.5% = 3.5pp**, worth **$756 a month**. It costs $4,000 a month to have. The model clears the offline criterion, clears the PRD's deciding number, and loses money by a factor of five.

**Decision: do not productionise the model. Keep the rule. Close the spike.**

And notice what just happened to the deciding number. "Complaints down 8%" was the right thing to agree in advance, and it turned out to be the wrong gate, because it never mentioned price. The lesson is not to abandon deciding numbers — it is that the gate is *value minus cost*, and a target expressed only in the business metric can be hit at a loss.

**One thing worth a week before closing it for good.** This whole calculation rests on $6 per complaint, which is the support cost. It ignores the customer who complains and then stops ordering. If a complaint actually costs $40 once churn is counted, the exposure is $144,000 a month, the increment is worth $5,040, and the model wins comfortably.

So the honest recommendation has two parts: **ship the rule now, and spend one week measuring what a late-delivery complaint does to that customer's next 90 days.** One number decides a $48,000-a-year question. What you must not do is assume the churn figure in either direction — module P6's rule against inventing numbers cuts both ways, and here it points at a cheap measurement instead of a guess.

**The memo, in four lines.** The spike answered its question in three weeks. The model beats the rule by 7 points of macro-F1 and by 3.5pp of complaints, which at the current cost of a complaint does not cover $4,000 a month of operation. The rule is live and delivering 5.5%. The case reopens if a complaint turns out to cost more than about $18 all-in — we are measuring that now.
</details>

## 📌 Remember

- One metric on a slide is a number without units. Demand the other bars: do nothing, the shipped rule, a human, and for forecasts, yesterday's value.
- The model does not compete against perfection. It competes against what is already running, and what is on trial is the increment.
- Clearing the spike's criterion makes the model a candidate, not a decision.
- The increment must pay for the machinery: inference, retraining, monitoring, on-call and the new failure mode — not the inference line alone.
- A deciding number expressed only in the business metric can be hit at a loss. The gate is value minus cost.
- Closing a project is a normal outcome. Write the memo yourself, include what would reopen the case, and never let an honest negative read as a failure — or every future result will be positive.
