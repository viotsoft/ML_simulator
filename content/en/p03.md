# Module P3. From “make it smarter” to a decision

> **Level:** PM Foundations · **Time:** ~45 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

Max forwards a thread from the catalogue team with one line of his own:

> "Victor liked it and wants it in the quarter. I have no idea what it means. Over to you."

The request, in full:

> **"Make search smarter — it should understand what people actually mean."**

The thread underneath has eleven replies, and every one of them describes a different feature. Someone means typo tolerance. Someone means synonyms. Someone means ranking by what sells. Someone means a chat box.

Lena is blunt about it at the sync:

> "We can build any of those. We cannot build all of them, and 'smarter' is not a thing we can be done with. Tell us what decision the model is making and we will tell you whether it is two weeks or two quarters."

## 🎯 Your task

1. Turn a wish into a decision that can be built, estimated and accepted.
2. Learn the four slots every ML feature has to fill before it enters a roadmap.
3. Recognise the two ways this goes wrong — the wish and the dashboard.

---

## 📚 Theory

### Every ML feature answers one question about one object at one moment

That sentence is the whole module. "Smarter search" is not a question. *"For this query, in this session, which 20 products do we put on the screen?"* is.

Once the question is that specific, everything downstream becomes possible: the team can estimate it, you can name a metric for it, and you can tell whether it worked. Until then, every estimate is a guess about a different feature.

### The four slots

Before an ML feature enters a roadmap, four slots have to be filled. If you cannot fill one of them, that is the thing to go and find out — not a detail to settle later.

**Object.** What single thing is the model making a statement about? A query. A transaction. An order. A seller's photo. If the answer is "the user experience", the slot is empty.

**Moment.** When exactly is the prediction made? As the user types. At checkout. Nightly, in a batch. This decides what data you are allowed to use — anything that only exists later is a leak, and module P11 shows what that costs.

**Decision.** What changes as a result? Products get reordered. A payment is held. A warning appears. If nothing changes, you are not building a feature, you are building a dashboard.

**Actor.** Who acts on it — the system automatically, or a person? A number shown to a moderator and a number that blocks a listing carry completely different risk, and the second one needs an appeal path.

### The two ways this goes wrong

**The wish.** "Smarter", "more personal", "better experience". These survive a roadmap review because nobody can object to them — and that is exactly the problem. A request nobody can disagree with is a request nobody can build. Your job is to make it specific enough to be disagreed with.

**The dashboard.** The opposite failure, and the sneakier one. The slots for object, moment and actor get filled, but the decision slot quietly holds "show the score". A churn probability displayed next to a customer's name changes nothing by itself. Someone has to call them, or an offer has to appear, or the number is decoration. Ask what a person does differently at 0.8 than at 0.3. If there is no answer, there is no feature.

### The screen is the proof

A useful test: sketch what changes on the screen. Not a design — a sentence. *"The top three results reorder."* *"A yellow banner appears above the order."* *"Nothing visible; the payment is held for 30 seconds and released."*

If you cannot write that sentence, the decision slot is not filled, whatever the brief says. And if what changes is "a number appears in an internal tool", you have found a dashboard wearing a feature's clothes.

## 💻 Practice

Fill the four slots for "make search smarter". There is more than one right answer — the point is that each one is a **different feature** with a different cost.

Produce three candidate versions, then say which you would take to Lena first and why.

<details>
<summary><b>Show a model answer</b></summary>

**Candidate A — ranking.** Object: one search query with its session. Moment: as results are returned. Decision: the order of the top 20. Actor: the system. *Metric: clicks and orders from search results.* This is the version the eleven replies were mostly circling.

**Candidate B — query understanding.** Object: the typed string. Moment: as the user types. Decision: which corrected or expanded query we actually search for. Actor: the system, visibly — "showing results for …" with a way back. *Metric: share of searches returning zero results.*

**Candidate C — nothing to do with ML.** Object: the query. Moment: on submit. Decision: whether to apply a synonym from a hand-kept list. Actor: the system. This is a rule, it ships in a week, and by module P2's test it should be tried first.

**Which one first: C, then A.** The zero-result searches are measurable today, a synonym list fixes a chunk of them in days, and what it does not fix becomes the evidence for funding A. Taking A straight to Lena means a quarter of work against a baseline nobody measured.

Notice what the exercise did: one unanswerable request became three answerable ones, and the cheapest turned out not to need the ML team at all.

## 📌 Remember

- Every ML feature answers one question about one object at one moment. If you cannot phrase that question, the feature is not ready for a roadmap.
- Four slots: object, moment, decision, actor. An empty slot is something to find out, not a detail for later.
- The moment decides which data you may use. Anything that only exists afterwards is a leak waiting to happen.
- A wish cannot be disagreed with, which is why it cannot be built. Make it specific enough to argue about.
- If nothing changes on the screen or in someone's behaviour, it is a dashboard, not a feature. Ask what a person does differently at 0.8 than at 0.3.
