# Module P19. Roadmapping under uncertainty

> **Level:** Senior ML PM · **Time:** ~55 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

Annual planning. Viktor needs a twelve-month plan for the board, and the board approves budgets against plans.

On your desk are four candidate bets. One is essentially a decision you already know how to make. One depends on data that three systems hold and nobody has ever joined. Two are somewhere in between.

Viktor is direct about the problem:

> "Last year you gave me a decision date instead of a delivery date for one project, and it worked. I cannot do that four times on one slide. The board will read four question marks as no plan at all."

He is right that four question marks is not a plan. He is wrong that the alternative is four dates.

## 🎯 Your task

1. Split the year into what you commit and what you fund in stages.
2. Fund uncertain bets in tranches, each ending in a decision.
3. Give leadership a calendar of when you will know, and write down the odds you are assuming.

---

## 📚 Theory

### A portfolio, not a plan

Every ML initiative contains two kinds of work, and module P7 already separated them at the project level. At the portfolio level the same split is what makes a year plannable: the rules, the interfaces, the instrumentation and the operational work can be committed with dates, and the searches cannot.

So the roadmap has two sections, and they are labelled differently. Not "confident" and "stretch" — **committed** and **funded to a decision**.

A tranche is module P7's spike applied per bet: a fixed budget, a question, a date, and a decision. The second tranche is not approved in advance. That is the whole mechanism — it converts "we might waste two quarters" into "we might waste two weeks, four times."

### Write down the odds

For each bet, record the probability you are assuming: *"I think this works with about 60% confidence."* Two reasons, both selfish.

It makes the portfolio arithmetic possible — a bet worth $6M at 30% is a better use of two weeks than a bet worth $900K at 50%, and you cannot see that without the numbers. And over a year of bets, your written-down probabilities become a record of your own calibration. A product manager whose 70%s come true about 70% of the time is worth far more than one who is always confident.

### Shape the portfolio

Do not fund four coin flips. A workable year has one thing that is nearly certain and small, two medium bets, and one long shot with a cheap first tranche. And it kills fast: the value of a portfolio comes as much from the bets you stop in week two as from the one that wins.

### How to say "we do not know yet"

Leadership does not need certainty. It needs to know **when it will know**, so that it can plan the conversation, the hiring and the next budget around that date. So replace the missing promise with a calendar, and be explicit about what each date produces:

> "On 14 February I will tell you whether the demand data can be joined at all. If it can, I will ask for a quarter. If it cannot, that bet is closed and the quarter goes to search."

That sentence is more useful to a board than any accuracy target, because it is a commitment you can actually keep.

### What not to do

- **Pad the estimate** so the uncertain work looks certain. You are not adding safety, you are hiding the gate — and the padding is spent, every time.
- **Promise the best case** because the room wants it. A year of this makes your dates worthless and your good news unbelievable.
- **Bury a risky bet inside a larger initiative** so it escapes scrutiny. It also escapes the gate, which is the only thing that would have stopped it at week two.

## 💻 Practice

Build the roadmap and write the paragraph for the board.

| Bet | Annual value | Your odds | Cost if fully funded | Main unknown |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fraud threshold and the vendor decision | $400K | 90% | 1 month, 1 person | none — it is a decision |
| Search re-ranking | $2.4M | 70% | 1 quarter, 3 people | does ranking move conversion |
| Demand forecasting for stocking | $6M | 30% | 2 quarters, 4 people | can three systems be joined at all |
| Support reply drafting | $900K | 50% | 6 weeks, 2 people | will agents accept the drafts |

<details>
<summary><b>Show a model answer</b></summary>

**Expected value, to order the queue.** Search $1.68M · demand $1.8M · drafting $450K · fraud $360K. Note that demand forecasting has the highest expected value *and* the lowest chance of working — which is an argument for a cheap first tranche, not for two quarters.

**The plan.**

| Bet | What is funded now | Gate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fraud threshold** | The whole thing. One month, committed, with a date. It is a decision, not a search — module P16's shadow month plus the threshold arithmetic. | none needed |
| **Search re-ranking** | Two weeks of data audit, then a three-week spike against the current ranking. | **28 March:** does the model beat today's ranking by the margin we wrote down? Then a quarter. |
| **Demand forecasting** | Two weeks. One question only: can the three systems be joined at a point in time, per warehouse, per day? | **14 February:** joinable or not. Nothing beyond this is committed. |
| **Support drafting** | One week, calling a model API rather than building — module P16's third option, used exactly as intended. Ten agents, real tickets, one question. | **7 February:** do agents accept the drafts? |

**Why demand forecasting gets two weeks and not two quarters.** At 30% odds, committing four people for half a year is a $3M bet on a data-engineering question that two weeks answers. If the join is impossible, the honest outcome is that we learned it in February and spent the quarter on search instead. If it is possible, the odds are no longer 30% and the bet gets re-priced with evidence.

**The paragraph for the board.**

> "One commitment and three decisions this quarter. The fraud threshold work ships in February and is worth about $400,000 a year; that number is not contingent on anything. Beyond that we have three bets, and we are buying answers rather than outcomes: on 7 February we will know whether support agents use AI-drafted replies, on 14 February whether our demand data can be joined at all, and on 28 March whether a model beats our current search ranking. Each answer costs one to five weeks. The largest of the three, warehouse stocking, is worth around $6M a year and I put its chance of working at roughly one in three — which is why we are spending two weeks on it and not a quarter. I will come back on 28 March with three answers and a request for the rest of the year."

**What that paragraph does.** It gives the board one number they can bank, three dates they can put in a calendar, an explicit probability they can hold you to, and a stated reason why the biggest opportunity is the smallest investment. Nowhere does it promise an accuracy, and nowhere does it hide a search inside a delivery date.

## 📌 Remember

- Two sections, labelled honestly: committed work with dates, and bets funded to a decision. Not "confident" and "stretch".
- Fund each uncertain bet in tranches, with the next tranche unapproved. That turns two wasted quarters into two wasted weeks, several times over.
- Write the probability down. It makes the portfolio comparable, and over a year it measures your own calibration.
- The highest expected value often belongs to the least likely bet. That is an argument for a cheap first tranche, not for a big commitment.
- Shape the portfolio: one near-certain and small, two medium, one long shot — and kill fast.
- Leadership needs to know when it will know. A calendar of decision dates is a commitment you can keep; an accuracy target is not.
- Do not pad, do not promise the best case, and do not hide a risky bet inside a larger initiative — hiding it from scrutiny also hides it from the gate.
