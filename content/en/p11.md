# Module P11. Reading an evaluation without being fooled

> **Level:** ML PM · **Time:** ~50 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

Igor brings the fraud model's evaluation to the review. One line, and the room relaxes.

> "AUC 0.97. Eighteen months of transactions, random 80/20 split."

Lena does not relax. She says the thing that experienced ML leads say when a number is too good:

> "0.97 on fraud is either a very good model or a leak. It is almost always a leak."

Igor is not hiding anything. He is a careful engineer who has been working alone for three weeks, and the mistake that produces 0.97 is quiet — it does not throw an error, it just quietly hands the model the answer. Somebody has to ask, and it does not have to be somebody who can read the code.

## 🎯 Your task

1. Ask four questions of any evaluation, without reading a line of code.
2. Recognise the signs of leakage that are visible from the outside.
3. Insist that the number is computed where the feature will actually run.

---

## 📚 Theory

### Four questions, in this order

1. **What data was it evaluated on?** Which period, which population, which channel.
2. **How was it split?** This is the question that catches most disasters.
3. **What is the baseline?** Module P10's bars. A number with no comparison is not a result.
4. **Which metric, at which threshold, on which population?** AUC is a summary over *all* thresholds; you ship exactly one.

None of these require expertise. They require asking.

### The split is where the money is lost

For anything that happens over time — and fraud, delays, churn and demand all happen over time — a random split puts January and March in training and February in the test set. The model gets to see the future around the cases it is tested on.

There is a second version of the same error that survives a time split: the same customer, or the same order, appearing on both sides. Ask directly — *"can the same customer be in training and in test?"*

### Signs of a leak that a product manager can see

- **The number is too good.** You know roughly how hard the problem is, because humans do it today and they are not at 0.97. A jump of that size is a claim that needs evidence.
- **A feature that knows the outcome.** Module P4's trap: ask for the top five features by importance and check each against "did this exist at the moment of the decision?" *Number of support contacts on this order* predicts a late delivery beautifully — after the delivery.
- **Performance does not fall on newer data.** Real models degrade on the most recent month, because the world has moved. A model that does equally well everywhere has usually been told something.
- **The test set is "the full dataset".** It contains the training data, so the score is partly a memory test.

### Insist on the number where the feature runs

Two mismatches to check every time.

**Threshold.** AUC averages over thresholds you will never use. Ask for precision and recall at the threshold you intend to ship, and the confusion matrix that goes with it. "0.97 AUC" and "at our threshold it flags 500 a day and 60 of them are real" are the same model, and only the second is a product statement.

**Population.** If the feature launches for mobile orders in three cities, the number that matters is the number on mobile orders in three cities. A model trained on everything and evaluated on everything can be strong overall and weak exactly where you ship it.

### Read twenty errors yourself

Ask for twenty cases the model got wrong, in plain language. This is the highest-value hour a product manager spends on a model. Patterns fall out that no aggregate shows: they are all first-time customers, or all from the warehouse that changed couriers, or all orders where the address was entered in a strange format. Some of those are bugs, some are scope decisions, and all of them are yours.

## 💻 Practice

Here is the whole evaluation slide. Write down what you ask.

> **Fraud model.** AUC 0.97. Trained on 18 months of transactions, random 80/20 split, evaluated on the full dataset. Top features: transaction amount, device age, **number of chargeback disputes for this customer**, hour of day.

<details>
<summary><b>Show a model answer</b></summary>

**Four things are wrong, and one of them is fatal.**

**1. The chargeback feature.** A dispute is filed *days after* the transaction. At the moment of the decision, this number does not exist — and for fraudulent transactions it is exactly what later gets disputed. The model has been handed the answer. This alone explains 0.97, and it is the whole finding.

> "Igor, at the moment we score the payment, has a chargeback been filed yet?"

That question, asked in a review, costs nothing and saves the launch. It is module P4's point-in-time rule applied to a feature-importance list.

**2. A random split on time-ordered data.** Even with the leaking feature removed, the score would be optimistic: fraud comes in waves, and a random split puts the same wave on both sides.

**3. "Evaluated on the full dataset."** That includes the 80% it learned from, so part of the score is a memory test. Ask for the number on held-out months only.

**4. No baseline and no threshold.** AUC 0.97 against what — the current rules engine? And at the threshold we would actually run, how many alerts a day and how many are real? Module P6's arithmetic needs those two numbers and cannot use AUC at all.

**What you ask for, in one message.**

> Four things before we go further: drop the chargeback feature and any other field that only exists after the transaction; re-split by time with the last three months held out; report precision and recall at the threshold we would ship, with the confusion matrix; and put the current rules engine on the same slide. Also, twenty examples it got wrong — I want to read them.

**And the tone, which matters.** Igor did not do anything careless; this specific mistake is the most common one in the field and it produces no error message. The way to ask is *"help me understand this feature"*, not *"this looks wrong"* — you need him to go looking for the next leak himself, on every project after this one.
</details>

## 📌 Remember

- Four questions, no expertise needed: what data, split how, against what baseline, and which metric at which threshold on which population.
- Production is always a split by time. A random split on time-ordered data measures a situation that will never occur.
- A number that is too good is a claim that needs evidence. You know roughly how hard the task is, because humans do it today.
- Check the top features against the moment of the decision. A field that only exists afterwards is the answer in disguise.
- AUC is not a product statement. Ask for precision and recall at the threshold you will ship, with the confusion matrix, on the population you will ship to.
- Read twenty errors in plain language. It is the highest-value hour you will spend on a model, and the patterns it reveals are product decisions.
