# Module P22. Agentic products

> **Level:** Advanced · **Time:** ~55 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

Support drafting has been live for two months. Agents accept 71% of drafts with no edit at all, and the queue is down by a third.

In the review, someone says the obvious thing:

> "If seven in ten go out untouched, why is a person clicking send?"

It is a fair question and the answer is not "because we are cautious". Something changes at that click, and it is not the quality of the text — it is who is answerable for it. Right now every message a customer receives was sent by a named employee who read it. Remove the click and the company has started talking to its customers by itself.

And a second proposal is already on the table: let the system also look up the order, check the refund policy, and issue the refund when the policy allows. Four steps instead of one.

## 🎯 Your task

1. Place the feature on the autonomy ladder deliberately, rung by rung.
2. Put gates on irreversibility and magnitude rather than on confidence.
3. Do the arithmetic of a multi-step chain before agreeing to one.

Most products belong on rung 2 or 3 for far longer than the team's enthusiasm suggests, and the reason is in the right-hand column rather than in the model.

### Blast radius is the question that decides

Before discussing rungs, answer one thing: **what is the worst thing one run can do, and how many runs happen before a person looks?** A wrong draft is one annoyed customer. A wrong refund is money. A wrong bulk price update, running unattended overnight, is a company-wide incident and a news story.

Blast radius, not accuracy, is what sets the rung. A 99%-accurate agent with an unbounded blast radius is a worse product than a 90%-accurate one that can only ever affect one customer per run.

### Gate on irreversibility and magnitude, not on confidence

Confidence gating is the intuitive design and the wrong one: the model is most confident exactly where it is most wrong about something it has never seen. Gate instead on properties of the **action**:

- Can the affected person undo it themselves? If not, gate it.
- Does it move money, access or data outside the company? Gate it.
- Is it irreversible in practice even if reversible in theory? A sent email can be retracted in no meaningful sense. Gate it.
- Is it a bulk action? Cap the number of items per run, always.

The same model can be allowed to act freely on reversible things and never on irreversible ones, and that is one product, not two.

### Design the undo before the action

If you cannot describe how a wrong action is reversed — by whom, in how long, with what the customer is told — then you have not finished designing the feature. This is module P9's rule for automated decisions, restated for a system that acts on its own: a wrong prediction should cost a click, and somebody has to have built the click.

### Multi-step is where products die

Errors compound, and the arithmetic is brutal in a way that intuition resists:

| Steps | At 95% per step | At 98% per step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 95% | 98% |
| 5 | 77% | 90% |
| 10 | 60% | 82% |
| 20 | 36% | 67% |

A four-step refund chain at 95% a step succeeds 81% of the time, which means **one run in five goes wrong somewhere** — and the failures are not uniform, they cluster in the unusual cases that most need a human.

The product answer is short chains with checkpoints, not better models. Break the chain where a cheap verification exists: after retrieving the order, confirm the order exists and belongs to this customer; after reading the policy, confirm the quoted rule is real. A verified step resets the multiplication.

### Log the trajectory, not the outcome

Module P13 asked for the inputs and the model version. An agent needs more: every step, every tool call, every intermediate result. When a refund is wrong, the question is not "what did it decide" but "at which of the four steps did it go wrong", and only the trajectory answers that. Without it, the first serious incident is unexplainable and the feature gets switched off permanently.

## 💻 Practice

Two proposals. Which rung for each, and what gates?

> **A.** The system sends the reply itself, with no agent click.
> **B.** The system looks up the order, reads the refund policy, decides whether the policy allows a refund, and issues it. Four steps.

<details>
<summary><b>Show a model answer</b></summary>

**A — rung 3, for a defined subset only.**

Not "71% acceptance means we can send". Split by what the message *does*:

- **Sent automatically:** replies that only convey facts already in the order record — where the parcel is, what the policy says, when the refund was processed. Blast radius: one customer, one informational message, correctable by a follow-up.
- **Stays on rung 2, a person clicks:** anything containing a commitment, a refund, an exception, an apology for a serious failure, or a reply to an angry ticket. These stay with a human not because the model writes them badly but because the cost of getting them wrong is not one annoyed customer.

Note that the gate is on **content type**, not on the model's confidence. The model is often most fluent exactly where it is inventing, which makes confidence the worst available gate.

Two mechanical requirements: a per-hour cap on automatic sends, so a bad prompt release cannot reach 6,000 customers before anyone notices, and a visible "a person wrote this" distinction, because trust in the channel is a company asset and spending it silently is a decision nobody made.

**B — not yet, and the reason is arithmetic rather than caution.**

Four steps at 95% each is 81%: **one refund decision in five goes wrong somewhere in the chain.** At the refund volumes from module P20 that is thousands of wrong outcomes a month, arriving as money moved incorrectly in both directions.

What makes it shippable is not a better model but a different shape:

1. **Verify after each step.** The order lookup is checked against the order service — does this order exist, does it belong to this customer. The policy citation is checked against the policy document, mechanically. Two verified steps stop compounding, and the chain becomes roughly two uncertain steps rather than four.
2. **Keep the decision itself on rung 3 with module P20's asymmetry.** The agent may *approve* a refund inside existing rules and a value cap; it may never *deny* one. Denial is the harmful direction and it stays with a person, exactly as in the capstone.
3. **A hard cap per run and per hour**, and a single flag that stops all of it in under a minute.
4. **The trajectory logged in full**, or the first wrong refund is unexplainable.

With those four, this is the Smart Refunds feature with a retrieval chain in front of it, and it gets the same defence and the same gate. Without them it is a system that moves money and is wrong one time in five.

**The sentence for the review.** *"Sending is a subset decision, not a threshold decision. And the four-step chain is not 95% accurate — it is 81% accurate, and that number is the whole conversation."*
</details>

## 📌 Remember

- The autonomy ladder — suggest, draft, act with a gate, act and notify, act silently — is a product decision, and each rung moves accountability away from a person who read it.
- Blast radius sets the rung, not accuracy. A 99% agent with unbounded reach is worse than a 90% one that can affect one customer per run.
- Gate on irreversibility, magnitude and bulk, not on confidence. The model is often most fluent precisely where it is inventing.
- If you cannot describe how a wrong action is undone, by whom and how fast, the feature is not designed yet.
- Errors compound: 95% a step is 60% over ten steps, and four steps is 81%. Short chains with cheap verification after each step, rather than better models.
- Cap actions per run and per hour, and keep one flag that stops everything in a minute.
- Log the whole trajectory. "Which step went wrong" is the only useful question after an incident, and only the trajectory answers it.
