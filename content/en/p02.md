# Module P2. Should this be ML at all?

> **Level:** PM Foundations · **Time:** ~50 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

Thursday. Max forwards you a thread from the business with four requests and one line of his own:

> "All four came in as 'can we use AI for this'. They are your call now. Lena says her team can take two things this quarter, not four."

The four requests:

1. **Risk.** "Block an order over 100,000 if the account was created less than a day ago."
2. **Growth.** "Recommendations on the home page — show each user what they are likely to buy."
3. **CRM.** "Send a 'we miss you' email to anyone who has not opened the app in 30 days."
4. **Catalogue.** "Check that the photo a seller uploads actually matches the category they chose."

Lena's team is the scarcest resource in the company. Two of these do not need them at all.

## 🎯 Your task

1. Separate the requests that need a model from the ones a rule closes.
2. Be able to justify each call in one sentence the requester will accept.
3. Learn the signal that tells you a rule has outlived itself.

---

## 📚 Theory

### The test

A rule is enough when **the condition is known in advance, can be written down, and does not drift**. Reach for a model when the decision depends on many weak signals whose weights you would have to discover from data rather than declare.

Say it out loud: *"Block if the amount is over 100,000 and the account is younger than a day."* That is a complete, checkable sentence. Now try the other one: *"Show what the user is likely to buy."* There is no sentence. There are millions of interactions whose combined weight nobody can write by hand. That is the line.

### What a model actually costs

The question is never "would a model be better". A model is almost always *slightly* better at something. The question is whether it is worth what it costs, and the costs are easy to miss because none of them appear in the first sprint.

- **Data.** It has to exist, be labelled, and be available at the moment the decision is made — not only in the warehouse afterwards. Module P4 is entirely this question.
- **Time.** Weeks, not days, and the estimate is less reliable than for ordinary work because part of it is research. Module P7 covers why.
- **Upkeep.** A model decays as the world moves. Someone owns retraining, monitoring and the pager, forever. Module P14 is that life.
- **Explanation.** A rule can be read out in a meeting. A model answer takes work to justify — and in regulated decisions, that work is mandatory.

Against all of that, a rule costs a few days and is auditable by anyone who can read.

### The rule that has outlived itself

Rules do not stay simple. The signal to watch is not size, it is **edit frequency**: when the rule needs a new exception every week, and nobody can predict what the next one will be, the condition has stopped being knowable in advance. That is precisely the situation a model exists for.

So the honest sequence is: ship the rule, instrument it, and let the exceptions tell you when to escalate. You get value in days instead of weeks, and by the time you build the model you have labelled data — the exceptions you were logging all along.

### Saying no without being the person who says no

"We are not using ML for that" is heard as a refusal. Replace it with a comparison the requester can check:

> "A rule gets you this in a week and we can explain every block to a customer. A model would take a quarter and we would still need the rule as a fallback. If the rule starts needing weekly exceptions, that is the trigger to revisit — and I will be watching for it."

You gave them something faster, you named the condition under which you would change your mind, and you took responsibility for watching that condition. That is a different conversation from no.

## 💻 Practice

Triage Max's four requests. For each: **rule or model**, and one sentence you would send back to the requester.

<details>
<summary><b>Show a model answer</b></summary>

**1. Block large orders from new accounts → rule.** The condition is a complete sentence, it is stable, and Risk owns it. It also has to be explainable to a blocked customer, which pushes further toward a rule. *"Shipping as a rule this sprint — and we will log every block so that if we start needing exceptions, we will know it is time for a model."*

**2. Home-page recommendations → model.** There is no sentence to write; the signal is millions of weak interactions. This is what models are for, and there is a clear metric and a clear baseline to beat — the current bestseller list. *"This is the one worth the team's quarter."*

**3. "We miss you" after 30 days → rule, and a badly framed one.** The rule is trivial. The real question hiding inside it is *whom it is worth contacting*, which is a churn-prediction problem — and the ML track already has that model. *"The email is a rule; if you want it to actually work, the interesting version is choosing who receives it, and that model already exists."*

**4. Photo matches category → model.** No writable condition; it needs perception. But note the sharp scoping question: for the top 20 categories with plenty of examples this is a solid project, and for the long tail it is not. Scope it before committing. *"Yes, and let us start with the 20 categories that cover most listings."*

**Result: two of four need the ML team**, which is exactly the capacity Lena has. The triage did not just answer the question — it resolved the resourcing conflict without a negotiation.
</details>

## 📌 Remember

- A rule is enough when the condition can be written as a sentence, stays true, and has an owner. Most requests fail the first test.
- Reach for a model when the decision rests on many weak signals whose weights must be learned rather than declared.
- A model's real cost is data, weeks, permanent upkeep and the burden of explanation — none of which appear in the first sprint.
- Edit frequency is the escalation signal: a rule that needs a new exception every week has become a model-shaped problem, and its exception log is your training data.
- Say no with a comparison and a named trigger for changing your mind, not with a verdict.
