# Module P17. Data as a product decision

> **Level:** Senior ML PM · **Time:** ~55 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

Three requests land on the same day.

Growth wants a thumbs-up / thumbs-down on every delay advisory: *"free labels, and it takes a day to build."*

Dmitry, counsel, has read the plan and asks a narrower question:

> "The transaction data you are using to train the fraud model — what did the customer agree to when they gave it to you? And how long are you keeping it?"

And Igor brings the uncomfortable one:

> "Our fraud model has been live five months and its measured precision keeps improving. I do not believe it. We block the transactions it is most sure about, so we never find out whether they were fraud. The model is being graded on the cases it let through."

The first is a small feature request. The second and third are the same subject as the first, and it is not a feature request at all.

## 🎯 Your task

1. Treat collection, consent and retention as product decisions with your name on them.
2. Tell a feedback loop that improves a model from one that quietly corrupts it.
3. Pay, deliberately, for the labels the product will never generate on its own.

---

## 📚 Theory

### Three questions before any collection

**Consent and purpose.** Data gathered so that a delivery can be completed does not automatically become training data for pricing. Purpose limitation is not a legal footnote; it is the reason to write down, in the PRD, what each dataset may be used for. "We already have it" is not a purpose.

**Retention.** The default of keeping everything for ever is a liability that grows without anyone deciding to grow it. Every additional month of raw personal data is more exposure in a breach and more to explain to a regulator, in exchange for training value that usually flattens out after a few months.

**Who can see it.** A dataset that every analyst can query is a dataset that will eventually appear in a spreadsheet on a laptop.

### The loop that helps, and the loop that lies

The virtuous version is the reason ML products compound: every use produces a label. A moderator confirms or overturns, a customer appeals, a delivery arrives late or on time — and next month's training data is better because the product ran.

The version that lies is more common than product managers expect.

There is a second poisoning mode with the same shape: the model's action changes the outcome it will be judged on. Orders that got a delay advisory were rescheduled, so they arrived on time, so they are labelled "not late" — and the model learns that the orders it warned about are fine.

### The only fix is to pay for a hole

You cannot reason your way out of missing data. The standard instrument is to **hold out a small random sample from the model's action**: let a fixed percentage of the cases you would have blocked go through, observe what happens, and treat that sample as the only unbiased measurement you have of the block decision.

It has a real cost, in fraud let through or violations published, and that cost is the price of knowing whether your model works. Price it, name it, and put it in the budget — because the alternative is a number that rises every quarter and means nothing.

### Free labels are not free of bias

The thumbs-up widget is genuinely cheap and genuinely useful, with one caveat that has to be written down next to the data: only a particular kind of person presses it. Appeals come from sellers who know the process; ratings come from people with a strong opinion. Use them, and never let them become the sole measure of anything.

### This is your decision, not the data team's

Every part of it — what is collected, what it may be used for, how long it is kept, and what you are willing to pay to see the cases you acted on — is a trade between value, cost and risk. That is the definition of a product decision, and the data team is entitled to an answer from you rather than a guess.

## 💻 Practice

Design how the company will learn about the transactions the fraud model blocks.

> 1.1M transactions a month, about 1,800 of them fraudulent. The model catches 62%, so it blocks roughly **1,116 real fraud attempts** and an unknown number of honest ones. A missed fraud costs **$220**. Today's exposure to missed fraud is about **$150,480 a month**.

<details>
<summary><b>Show a model answer</b></summary>

**1. A 2% hold-out, priced openly.**

Two per cent of the transactions the model would block are allowed through, chosen at random, and their outcome is observed. That is roughly 22 fraudulent transactions a month reaching completion: **about $4,840 a month**, or **3.2%** of what missed fraud already costs us.

For that, we get the only unbiased answer to three questions we currently cannot answer at all: what share of blocked transactions were actually fraud, how that share is changing, and what a threshold change would really do. Without it, module P6's arithmetic is being run on numbers from the cases the model already thought were safe.

**2. The honest-block rate, from the same sample.** The hold-out also measures the error we care most about: how many of the people we block were doing nothing wrong. There is no other way to get this number, because a blocked honest customer usually just leaves.

**3. Appeals, used carefully.** Free, immediate, and biased towards customers who know how to complain. Good for finding *patterns* — a device type, a city, a payment method — and unusable as a rate.

**4. The consent and retention answer for Dmitry.** In the PRD: the purpose is fraud prevention on this product; the data is not used for pricing or marketing without a separate basis; raw transaction detail is retained 13 months and aggregates thereafter; access is limited to the fraud team and audited. If someone later wants this data for a different purpose, that is a new decision with its own paperwork — and a written purpose is what makes that conversation possible instead of automatic.

**5. What you tell Igor about the rising precision.** He is right and the metric is not trustworthy. From the day the hold-out starts, precision is reported on the hold-out sample only, with a note on the dashboard saying so. The old series stays on the chart with a line marking the change, because a metric that silently changes definition is worse than either version of it.

**And the sentence for the budget conversation with Marina.** *"$4,840 a month is not a loss. It is what it costs to know whether a $150,000-a-month system is working, and it is the cheapest line in this entire feature."*
</details>

## 📌 Remember

- Collection, purpose, retention and access are product decisions. "We already have it" is not a purpose, and keeping everything for ever is a liability nobody chose.
- A feedback loop compounds when every use produces a label — and lies when the action prevents the outcome, or changes it.
- You only observe outcomes for the cases you let through. Rejected applicants never default.
- The fix is a priced hole: a small random hold-out from the model's action, treated as the only unbiased measurement of that decision.
- Labels the product generates are cheap and biased towards the people who bother. Use them for patterns, never as a rate.
- A metric whose definition changes silently is worse than either version. Mark the change on the chart.
