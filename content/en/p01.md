# Module P1. First week on an ML team

> **Level:** PM Foundations · **Time:** ~45 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

Monday, 10:00. You are the new product manager at **Datacore** — a marketplace with 2 million active users and a fintech arm. You have shipped software products for years. What is new is that three of the features you now own are powered by models.

Victor, the CTO, keeps the introduction short:

> "Max runs the marketplace roadmap. You take the ML surface: recommendations, the fraud checks the wallet runs, and the delivery-time promise we show at checkout. Lena leads the ML team, Igor is her senior, Anya owns the data. They are good. What we have been missing is someone who can say what these features are supposed to *do* for the business — and say no when the answer is nothing."

Your first meeting is the recommendations review. A slide says:

> **Model quality: 94.7% accuracy. Ready for launch.**

Everyone nods. You are about to learn that this sentence, on its own, means almost nothing — and that asking why is your job, not a display of ignorance.

## 🎯 Your task

1. Understand what a model actually promises, and why "94.7%" is not a commitment you can put in front of a business stakeholder.
2. Learn the three things that change about your job when a feature is probabilistic.
3. Take away four questions to ask in any first meeting about an ML feature.

---

## 📚 Theory

### A model returns a probability, not a fact

An ordinary feature is deterministic. "When the user taps Pay, charge the card and show the receipt." You can write that down, test it, and it is either correct or a bug.

A model is different in kind. It takes an input it has never seen, compares it to patterns learned from history, and returns a number: *0.83 that this transaction is fraud*, *0.12 that this customer churns this month*. Nothing about that number is a promise about the individual case. It is a statement about a population.

Three consequences follow, and all three land on the product manager.

**The output has to be turned into a decision.** A probability is not a feature. Someone has to decide what happens at 0.83 and what happens at 0.42 — block, hold for review, or let through. That threshold is a product decision with a price tag attached, not a technical detail. It is where most of the value of an ML feature is won or lost, and module P6 is entirely about it.

**Being wrong is not a bug.** A well-built model is wrong on a known fraction of cases, forever. If your acceptance criterion is "it should not make mistakes", the feature can never ship. Your criterion has to name how often and *in which direction* you can live with being wrong.

**Quality is only real after launch.** Offline numbers are a filter, not a verdict. Until it meets live traffic you do not know what the feature does to the business — which is why module P12 is about the experiment, and why "the model scored well" is never the same sentence as "the feature works".

### What "94.7% accuracy" leaves out

Accuracy is the share of predictions that were right. It sounds like a grade. It hides three things a product manager needs.

**It hides which errors.** Blocking an honest customer and letting a fraudster through are both "errors" to accuracy. They are not remotely the same to the business — one costs a support call, the other costs the money in the account.

**It hides the base rate.** If fraud is 0.8% of transactions, a model that answers "not fraud" every single time scores 99.2% and catches nobody. That is not a hypothetical; it is exactly what happened to a team next door, and the ML track walks through the post-mortem.

**It hides the decision.** Accuracy is computed at some threshold, usually 0.5, chosen by default rather than on purpose. Change the threshold and the number changes. Nobody on the slide mentioned which one they used.

### The question that reframes the meeting

The useful question is never "how accurate is it". It is:

> **What decision does this prediction drive, and what does each kind of mistake cost us?**

That question does three things at once. It forces the conversation from the model to the product. It makes the cost of errors explicit while the feature can still be changed. And it tells the team which metric to optimise, because "which errors hurt more" is exactly the information an optimisation needs.

### Four questions for any first meeting

1. **What decision does this drive?** If nothing changes as a result of the prediction, there is no feature — only a dashboard.
2. **Who acts on it — a person or the system?** A number shown to a human and a number that blocks a payment carry completely different risk.
3. **What happens when it is wrong, in each direction?** Ask for both. Teams volunteer one.
4. **What do we compare it against?** If there is no current rule or human process to beat, you cannot tell whether the model is worth its cost. Module P10 turns this into a gate.

None of these require you to know how the model is built. All four are things only you can decide.

## 💻 Practice

The recommendations team sends you their one-page brief. Three lines from it are below, written the way an ordinary feature would be written.

**Rewrite each one so it can actually be accepted.** For each line, name the decision, the direction of error you care about, and a criterion you could check after launch rather than before.

> **Line 1.** "The recommendation block shows products the user wants to buy."
>
> **Line 2.** "The model must be at least 95% accurate."
>
> **Line 3.** "Wrong recommendations are not acceptable."

<details>
<summary><b>Show a model answer</b></summary>

**Line 1 → a decision, not a wish.** "For a user with at least one session in the last 30 days, the block shows 20 products ranked by the model; for everyone else it falls back to the category bestsellers." Now there is a rule you can build, and an explicit answer for the cold-start case — the one the original line silently assumed away.

**Line 2 → a business metric with a comparison.** "Revenue per session from the block beats the current bestseller list by at least 8% in an A/B test, with no drop in overall conversion." Accuracy is not in the sentence, and it should not be: the number that decides go or no-go is the one finance recognises. The guardrail is what stops a win on one metric being a loss overall — module P12 goes into this.

**Line 3 → an affordable error rate, in the direction that matters.** "An irrelevant recommendation costs us attention and nothing else, so we optimise for the top 3 slots being useful and accept weaker tail suggestions. A recommendation for an out-of-stock or restricted item is never acceptable and is filtered before display, not learned." Two kinds of "wrong", two very different tolerances — and the second is a rule, not a model.

The pattern in all three: **move from a wish about the model to a decision about the product**, then attach the cost of being wrong to each direction.
</details>

## 📌 Remember

- A model returns a probability about a population, never a fact about the case in front of it. Turning that number into a decision is product work.
- A single accuracy figure hides which errors were made, how rare the event is, and which threshold produced it. Ask what it hides before you quote it.
- "It should not make mistakes" is an acceptance criterion no model can meet. Name how often and in which direction you can afford to be wrong.
- Offline metrics filter candidates; the experiment decides. A feature is not proven until live traffic has seen it.
- The four questions — what decision, who acts, what each error costs, what we beat — need no ML knowledge and are the part only you can supply.
