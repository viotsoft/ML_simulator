## 🏢 The situation

Monday, 10 a.m. You are the new product manager at **Datacore**, a marketplace with 2 million active users and a fintech arm of its own. You have run products for years. Exactly one thing is new: the three features you now own all run on models.

Viktor, the CTO, keeps the introduction short.

> "Max runs the marketplace roadmap. You get everything that runs on a model: recommendations, wallet fraud detection, and the delivery-time promise at checkout. Lena leads the ML team, Igor is her senior engineer, Anya owns the data. They are strong technically. What we were missing is someone to answer a different question: what is all of this supposed to change for the customer and the business?"

Your first meeting starts in an hour.

By the end of this module you will be able to do four things, and all four follow from that scene.

1. Tell a model's prediction apart from the product's decision.
2. Stop treating a single offline metric as proof of value.
3. See that the two errors cost different amounts, and insist on knowing how much.
4. Demand a baseline, without which a model cannot be judged at all.

## 📚 Theory

### 1. The model does not make the business decision

Ordinary product logic is deterministic: the customer taps "Pay" → the system charges the card → it shows a receipt. The same input gives the same result.

An ML feature works differently. The model takes an input and returns a number: `fraud_score = 0.83`, `churn_probability = 0.71`, `predicted_delivery_time = 37 min`. None of those values is a product action on its own.

It follows that **a prediction is an estimate, not a fact**. `fraud_score = 0.83` does not mean "this transaction is definitely fraudulent". It means: given this transaction's features, the model rates the risk as high. Which is why "the model must not make mistakes" is unusable as a requirement in production, and the usable question is: which errors do we accept, how often, and at what cost?

### 2. A model does not always return a probability

| What the model returns | What the product does with it |
| --- | --- |
| a probability | compare it against a threshold and pick an action |
| a class | route cases down different branches of the flow |
| a ranking score | sort, and show the first N |
| a number: ETA, revenue, demand | turn it into a promise or into a plan |
| text or an image | show it to a person, or hand it to them for approval |

So the right question is not "what is the model's probability" but **what exactly does it return, and how does the product use that output**.

### 3. A model error is not the same thing as a bug

| | False alarm (FP) | Miss (FN) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| fraud | an honest customer cannot pay, calls support, conversion drops | the money is gone, a chargeback follows, risk costs rise |
| delivery time | we promised 60 minutes and delivered in 38 — the order may never have been placed | we promised 30 and delivered in 48 — the promise is broken |

To accuracy, both are simply "a wrong prediction". To the business they are two different events with two different price tags.

<div class="iq-num">Scene 2 · the delivery promise</div>

The model forecasts `ETA = 37 min`. The product decision here is not "the model said 37" but **what promise we show the customer**: 37, 35–40, 40, or 45 minutes.

Show 30 and deliver in 48, and you get a disappointed customer, a support contact, cancellations and a dent in trust. Show 60 when you could have managed 38, and the customer may not place the order at all. So the product manager settles not only how accurate the forecast is, but **which direction of error costs the business more**. And that, in turn, shapes which loss function, interval or decision rule is worth using.

### 4. Why "94.7% accuracy" says almost nothing

Three questions back, each of which changes what the number means.

1. **94.7% of what?** Accuracy, precision, recall, ROC-AUC, NDCG, RMSE, MAE? "Quality = 94.7%" is almost useless on its own.
2. **On which data?** Train, validation, a held-out test, last month, a single region, new users or returning ones? The same number can mean completely different things.
3. **Compared with what?** If the current system scores 96.2%, the new model is worse than what already runs.

A baseline can be the current model, a business rule, a heuristic, a manual process, a vendor's solution, top sellers, a plain average — or doing nothing at all.

The current baseline is the top 20 bestselling products in the category. The new model personalises the order, and offline NDCG@10 is up 11%. That is good news, and it is not yet a reason to roll out to 100%.

The right next question is **what hypothesis we are testing online**. For example: a personalised ranking raises revenue per eligible session by at least 5% against the bestseller baseline. With guardrails: overall conversion does not fall by more than 1%, return rate does not rise, p95 latency stays inside its SLA.

<div class="advice">

**Be careful with revenue attributed to the recommendation block.** A recommender can simply move a purchase from somewhere else in the interface. What matters is the incremental effect on the business, not the widget's own success.

</div>

### 5. The threshold is part of the decision logic

| score | what the product does |
| --- | --- |
| 0.00–0.60 | approve |
| 0.60–0.90 | send to manual review |
| 0.90–1.00 | block |

Move the blocking boundary from 0.95 to 0.70 and the system catches more potential fraud while blocking more honest payments. The threshold trades one error for the other.

And it is not an ML parameter. An ML engineer will give you precision, recall, ROC and the confusion matrix — but the decision also depends on the cost of fraud, the cost of a manual review, conversion, support costs, regulatory risk and UX. Choosing the operating point is a joint decision between product, ML, risk and the business, and the product manager is accountable for the trade being stated out loud. Module P6 goes into it properly.

One last thing in the theory: **an offline metric is not product success**. NDCG@10 up 7% is a good signal, but it is not "revenue up 7%".

| Level | Examples | The question it answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| offline quality | NDCG, precision, recall, MAE, RMSE | is this worth taking into an experiment at all? |
| behaviour in the product | CTR, conversion, manual review rate | are people reacting the way we expected? |
| business outcome | revenue per session, fraud losses, margin | scale it, or switch it off? |

That pyramid is the whole subject of module P5. Here one thing is enough: **accuracy is not a KPI**.

## 💻 Practice

### 💼 PM Framework: ML Feature First Review

Thirteen lines. Copy them somewhere of your own — these are what you fill in at the first meeting about any ML feature. Here they are filled in with wallet fraud detection: the same system you will take apart in the challenge.

| Field | Example: wallet fraud detection |
| --- | --- |
| **Business problem** | the wallet loses money to fraudulent payments and manual review cannot keep up with the volume |
| **Model output** | a fraud score from 0 to 1 on every transaction |
| **Decision** | above 0.95 block; 0.75–0.95 send to manual review; below that, approve |
| **Who acts** | the system blocks on its own; in the middle band a risk analyst decides |
| **Action** | the payment is rejected, or held until it has been reviewed |
| **Error 1 — FP** | an honest payment is blocked |
| **Its cost** | a support contact, lost conversion, and sometimes a customer who leaves |
| **Error 2 — FN** | a fraudulent payment goes through |
| **Its cost** | $420 of average loss plus a chargeback |
| **Current baseline** | manual review of 4% of transactions under the risk team's rules |
| **Offline metric** | precision and recall at the threshold we will actually run — not accuracy |
| **Online product metric** | share of honest payments blocked, time to a decision per transaction |
| **Business metric** | fraud losses plus the cost of reviews, per month |

### Three requirements to rewrite

The recommendations team has sent you a brief. Three of its lines are written the way requirements for an ordinary software feature get written. **Rewrite each one as a requirement for an ML product**: name the model output, the decision, the error you care about, the baseline, the offline gate and the online success criterion.

> **Line 1.** "The recommendation block shows products the user wants to buy."
>
> **Line 2.** "Model accuracy must be at least 95%."
>
> **Line 3.** "Wrong recommendations are unacceptable."

<details>
<summary><b>Show a model answer</b></summary>

**Line 1 → a decision, not a wish.** "For users with at least one session in the past 30 days, the system ranks available products by recommendation score and shows the top 20 after business filters are applied. Users with no history get the baseline: the bestselling products of the relevant category."

What changed: there is now a concrete decision rule, a defined fallback for cold start, a baseline, ML is responsible only for the ordering, and hard constraints have moved into rules.

**Line 2 → two different gates.** One number is not enough here; you need two.

*The offline gate:* "the new model's NDCG@10 is at least 5% above the current baseline on a held-out set, at coverage of no less than 80%." This answers whether the model is worth taking into an experiment.

*The online success criterion:* "in an A/B test, revenue per eligible session rises by at least 5% against the bestseller baseline." Guardrails: overall conversion does not fall by more than 1%, return rate does not rise, p95 latency stays inside its SLA. This answers a different question — whether the feature creates real value.

**Line 3 → two different situations hiding under the word "wrong".**

A user was recommended a product they are not interested in: an ordinary ranking error, acceptable in some share of cases. A user was shown a product that is out of stock or not permitted to them: that is not a ranking error, it is a breach of a hard constraint.

A correct version: "The model optimises the usefulness of the top positions and may produce irrelevant items in an acceptable share of cases. Products that are out of stock, prohibited for the user, or unavailable in their region are never displayed and are filtered out by business rules before the final list is assembled."

Common to all three: **move from a wish about the model to a decision about the product**, then attach the cost of an error to each direction.

</details>

## 🔥 Final challenge

You arrive at the fraud model's review. The team shows seven numbers.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Fraud prevalence | 0.7% |
| Accuracy | 99.5% |
| Precision | 91% |
| Recall | 38% |
| Currently under manual review | 4% of transactions |
| Average loss from missed fraud | $420 |
| Cost of one manual review | $3.70 |

Lena says:

> "If we raise the threshold, precision goes to 96% and recall drops to 26%. I suggest we do that — there will be fewer false positives."

Answer as the product manager.

1. What decision is the system making, and who acts?
2. What does each of the two errors cost?
3. Are these numbers enough to choose a threshold?
4. What are we comparing the model against?
5. Which business metric would you take into an experiment?

<details>
<summary><b>Show a model answer</b></summary>

**Work it per 100,000 transactions.** That is 700 fraudulent ones. At 38% recall, 266 are caught and 434 missed; at 91% precision, 292 are flagged, 26 of them honest.

| | flagged | missed fraud | review |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| today (P 91% / R 38%) | 292 | 434 × $420 = **$182,280** | 292 × $3.70 = $1,082 |
| the proposal (P 96% / R 26%) | 190 | 518 × $420 = **$217,560** | 190 × $3.70 = $701 |

**"Fewer false positives" saves $380 and costs $35,280.** That is 92 to 1 against the proposal. It answers question 2, and it settles question 1 along the way: the system blocks on its own, so the price of an error is not the ML engineer's to choose.

**The numbers are not enough — question 3.** $3.70 is an analyst's time, not the cost of a blocked honest payment: that customer did not pay, called support, and may not come back. Without that figure the trade cannot be computed, and it has to be gone and fetched — support has the first half, the second will be your judgement, written down as a judgement.

**We are not comparing against "do nothing" — question 4.** The baseline here is manual review of 4% of transactions, and nobody said how much fraud it catches. Note something else too: a model that always answers "not fraud" scores 99.3% accuracy on this data. The slide shows 99.5% — a win over that by two tenths of a point, while catching 38% of the fraud.

**The experiment's metric — question 5:** fraud losses plus the cost of reviews per month, as one number. Guardrail: the share of honest payments blocked does not rise.

</details>

## 📌 Remember

- **Four questions for any first meeting:** what decision is being made, who acts, what happens on an error in each direction, and what we compare against. None of them needs ML knowledge, and all four are yours to bring.
- A model returns a prediction or a score, not a business decision. The product begins where that number becomes a decision and an action.
- One ML metric proves nothing without context: which metric, on which data, against which baseline, at which threshold.
- Errors almost always cost different amounts. FP and FN are not the same thing, and the product manager has to get an explicit answer on what each side costs.
- Not everything is solved by a model: prediction belongs to ML, hard constraints to rules, and the action to the decision layer.
- A baseline is mandatory. The model does not need to be good; it needs to be better than the way this decision is made today.

**Further reading:** Google's *Rules of Machine Learning*, the first rules — on baselines, simple solutions and measurable systems. In the ML track: classification metrics, the confusion matrix, precision against recall, ranking metrics and threshold selection.
