# Module P13. Launch readiness

> **Level:** ML PM · **Time:** ~50 minutes · **Format:** situation → theory → practice → quiz

---

## 🏢 The situation

Version two of the delay advisory won its experiment. Lena sends a one-line message:

> "The model is ready. When do we launch?"

You are about to answer "Monday" when the operations lead adds a comment to the thread:

> "Quick question. At three in the morning, when this starts flagging every order in the country, who do I call and what do I switch off?"

Nobody has an answer. Not because the team was careless, but because "ready" has meant *the model performs* for the whole project, and from today it has to mean something much less interesting: *this can be operated by people who were not in the room.*

## 🎯 Your task

1. Roll out in stages, and know what each stage can actually catch.
2. Demand a runbook and a rollback that is a switch rather than a deploy.
3. Launch the feature to the support team before you launch it to customers.

---

## 📚 Theory

### "Ready" is a checklist, not a number

The metric is necessary and was settled two modules ago. What is left is entirely operational, and it is the product manager's list because every item on it is about consequences for people.

### Three stages, and each one catches something different

That failure has a name — training/serving skew — and it is worth knowing as a product manager for one reason: it is invisible in every offline number, and it is the most common cause of a model that scored well and then behaved oddly in production.

### Rollback is a switch, not a deploy

If turning the feature off requires a build, a review and a deploy, then your worst hour is forty minutes long. The requirement is plain: **a flag anyone on call can flip, without an engineer, without a release, with an effect within a minute** — and the same for the threshold, which module P8 already put in the PRD.

Write down who is allowed to flip it and on what evidence. An off switch nobody feels authorised to use at 3 a.m. is not an off switch.

### The runbook, and it is yours to demand

Six lines, and it exists before launch, not after the first incident:

- **What "broken" looks like in numbers.** *"More than 8% of orders flagged in an hour, or the fallback rate above 5%."* Not "if it seems wrong".
- **What alerts fire, and to whom.** Including the ones that are not engineering alerts: a jump in the flag rate is a business alert.
- **The fallback behaviour**, and confirmation it has been tested by actually turning the model off.
- **How to disable it** — the switch, the person, the expected effect.
- **What support tells customers** while it is off.
- **Who decides** to re-enable, and on what evidence.

### Support launches first

The people who will receive the complaints must know about the feature before the customers do. One page: what it does, what it looks like when it is wrong, how a customer appeals, and the exact sentence to say. A support team learning about a new automated decision from an angry customer is an avoidable, self-inflicted incident.

### Three things nobody asks for and every launch needs

- **Log the prediction with its inputs and the model version.** Without this, a complaint six weeks from now is unanswerable: you cannot tell what the model saw, or which model it was. This is the single most expensive omission to fix later.
- **A version label on every prediction.** Otherwise "did this change on Tuesday" has no answer.
- **The appeal path built, not designed.** Module P9's one-click disagreement, in code, with somewhere for it to go.

## 💻 Practice

Here is the state of the launch. What do you refuse to go live without, and what can follow next sprint?

> 1. Offline evaluation clean, split by time. ✅
> 2. The experiment won on the primary metric, guardrails held. ✅
> 3. The model is served behind a feature flag. ✅
> 4. The threshold is a constant in the model service. ❌
> 5. Predictions are logged — the score only, no inputs, no model version. ❌
> 6. No shadow run was done. ❌
> 7. Alerting: error rate and latency, owned by engineering. ✅ Nothing on the flag rate or on complaints. ❌
> 8. Rollback requires a deploy, about 40 minutes. ❌
> 9. Support has not been briefed. ❌
> 10. The appeal path is in the design file, not in the code. ❌

<details>
<summary><b>Show a model answer</b></summary>

**Blocking — five of them.**

- **(8) Rollback by deploy.** Everything else on this list is survivable if you can stop it in a minute. There is already a feature flag in item 3; the rollback must use it. Cheap to fix, and it changes the size of every other risk on the page.
- **(5) No inputs, no version in the logs.** The first serious complaint is unanswerable without this, and back-filling it is impossible — the data was never written. This is the item that is cheap today and unfixable in retrospect.
- **(9) Support not briefed.** One page and thirty minutes. Launching an automated customer-facing decision to an unaware support team is self-inflicted.
- **(10) The appeal path.** From module P9: a decision the affected person cannot contest is not acceptable at any accuracy. It is not a phase-two item — it is part of the feature.
- **(7) No alert on the flag rate.** Error rate and latency tell you the service is up. They say nothing about a model that is up and flagging 40% of orders. The runbook's numeric definition of "broken" needs an alert behind it.

**Not blocking, with reasons.**

- **(4) The hardcoded threshold.** It should be configurable and it goes in next sprint. Today it is survivable, because the flag can turn the whole thing off, which is the decision that actually matters at 3 a.m.
- **(6) No shadow run.** This is the interesting one, and the answer is that **the shadow stage has already effectively happened.** The experiment ran for three weeks on live traffic through the real serving pipeline — which is exactly the thing shadow mode exists to test. Demanding it here is ceremony. Say so explicitly, and name the reason, so that nobody concludes the stage is optional in general: the next model that goes live without an experiment does need it.

**The launch plan.**

> Canary at 5% on Tuesday, with the flag-rate alert live and the rollback on the flag. Full ramp on Friday if the guardrail holds. Support brief goes out Monday; the appeal endpoint ships with the canary. Configurable threshold next sprint.

**And the answer to the operations lead**, which is what started this: *at 3 a.m. you flip `delay_advisory` to off in the config dashboard, it takes effect within a minute, the page renders without the advisory, and you wake nobody. You tell me in the morning.*
</details>

## 📌 Remember

- "Ready" stops being a metric at this point and becomes a checklist about operating the thing — which makes it yours.
- Three stages answer three questions: shadow catches live inputs differing from training inputs, canary caps exposure while real people meet it, full ramp reveals slow effects.
- Skipping a stage is allowed when something earlier answered its question. Say which, and why.
- Rollback is a flag anyone on call can flip in a minute, without an engineer. An off switch nobody feels authorised to use is not one.
- Log every prediction with its inputs and the model version. It is cheap today and impossible to reconstruct later.
- Brief support before customers arrive, and ship the appeal path with the feature rather than after it.
